\advsect Rates of change

\datestamp{31}{viii}
This set of notes assumes that the reader is familiar with all the pre-calculus concepts that one learns
in the later years of secondary school:~functions, trigonometry, exponential functions, logarithms, etc.
However, given that the very definition of what differential calculus is requires us to talk about functions
and their inputs, it is perhaps prudent to begin with a refresher on real-valued functions with real-valued
outputs.

A {\it function} is a collection of ordered pairs $(x,y)$ with the rule that if $(x,y)$
and $(x,z)$ are both in the collection, then $y=z$. We can view $x$ as the {\it input} to the function,
and $y$ as the {\it output} of the function; under this view, the rule above is simply that the function
never outputs two different values when given the same input. People like to use the letter
$f$ (or $g$ or $h$, but really we can use any letter) to denote a function,
and $f(x)$ to denote its value evaluated at a given input $x$. However, many particular functions are
difficult to write without involving the input variable $x$ in the expression,
and in these cases we'll write $f(x)$ to mean the function as well.

Here are some examples of functions:
\medskip
\item{a)} The function $f(x) = 3x$, defined on all real numbers, that outputs triple its input value.
\smallskip
\item{b)} The function that assigns to each real number $x$ its square $x^2$.
\smallskip
\item{c)} The function $y=f(x)$ that equals $0$ whenever $x$ is a negative real number, and equals $x$
whenever $x\ge 0$.
\smallskip
\item{d)} The function that takes as input any nonzero real number $x$ and outputs its reciprocal $1/x$.
\smallskip
\item{e)} The function $f(x)$, defined on all real numbers, given by
$$f(x) = \cases{ 1, & if $x$ is rational;\cr 0, & if $x$ is irrational.}$$
\medskip
Note that in each case above, we were careful to specify what the possible inputs to the function were. Going back
to the definition of a function as a collection of ordered pairs $(x,y)$, the set of all $x$ such
that $(x,y)$ is in the function is called the {\it domain} of $f$; this is precisely the set of all valid
inputs to the function $f$. (The set of all $y$ such that $(x,y)$
is in $f$ is called the {\it range} of $f$, but this definition won't be as important to us.)

The title of this section is ``rates of change,'' but determining the rate at which a given function changes
is actually what this {\it entire class}
will essentially be about. We want to study how much the output of a function changes when you change its input.

For example, consider a car moving from Montreal to Quebec City. As time goes on, it gets further away from
Montreal and closer to Quebec City. So we can model the trip as a function $d(t)$, where for each positive
real number $t$ we let $d(t)$ equal the distance of the car from Montreal (in kilometres),
exactly $t$ minutes since it
set off. The rate of change of this function at any given point in time $t$ is the car's {\it velocity} at that
point.

How do we calculate velocity? Well, we know that it is measured in units such as ``kilometres per hour''
or ``metres per second,'' so it is not too hard to see that velocity is calculated by dividing distance
over time.

Let's go back to the car example. Suppose your car is equipped with a very good GPS that can measure your
distance from Montreal; however, it does not have a working speedometer. Your friend (who is driving) asks
you, ``How fast am I driving?'' You have taken high school physics, so you know how to solve this problem.
You get out a stopwatch and start it exactly when the GPS measures your distance at $135.2$ kilometres
from Montreal. After one
minute on the stopwatch, the GPS reads $137.4$, so you report to your friend that she is driving
$137.4-135.2 = 2.2$ kilometres per minute, or $132$ kilometres per hour.

But your friend is not satisfied. ``You told me my average speed over the course of a minute, not my speed
at the moment I asked you the question. During that minute, I had to speed up to overtake a U-Haul and then
slow down because someone cut me off in the fast lane.''

So you try and do better. You get out the stopwatch again, and when you start it the GPS reads $137.7$.
You wait ten seconds, then observe that the GPS reads $138.1$. So you report your friend's new speed at
$138.1-137.7 = 0.4$ kilometres every ten seconds, or $2.4$ kilometres per minute, or $144$ kilometres per
hour.

Your friend is a little more satisfied, but wonders if you can do better. So you try one last time. You
start the stopwatch when the GPS reads $138.5$, wait exactly one second, and then check the GPS again.
To everyone's surprise and disappointment, it still reads $138.5$, because it only measures distance to the
tenth part of a kilometre. So the calculation would report
that the car has moved $0$ kilometres in that one second, that is, it's going $0$ kilometres per hour.

Obviously in this example you were hamstrung by the precision of your measuring instruments, but it is still
worth pondering the following point. Your friend was asking for her speed at some particular instant.
However, during any particular instant she does not move, nor does time move. So any na{\"\i}ve calculation
as to her speed would go along the lines of $0/0$, which is an undefined value.

Yet it is undeniable that something's ``speed at a particular instant'' is a concept that holds meaning
in everyday life. If you are in a plane and your friend is in a car below you, then it's certainly
not nonsensical to say that you are moving faster than your friend at that moment, even though technically it
could also be said that both of you have moved ``$0$ kilometres over $0$ hours.''

The way we make this precise is suggested by your technique in the first scenario. To avoid dividing by zero,
we let time elapse just a tiny bit, then measure how much the object's position has changed in that minute
interval, and perform the division. Assuming we have arbitrarily sophisticated measuring instruments,
we can keep taking smaller and smaller intervals, and arrive at something closer and closer to the
``instantaneous velocity.'' But it is worth stating that ``instantaneous velocity'' is a theoretical concept,
and not one that can be measured exactly. So when you are driving in a real car, and your speedometer
reads some value, say, $127$ kilometres per hour, what it's really doing is measuring your car's
change in distance over a very small period of time, and reporting to you the quotient (not unlike what
you were doing in that first scenario!).

\medskip\boldlabel Secants and tangents.
The average rate of change of a function $f(x)$
between the input $x$ and the input $x+h$ (for
some small $h>0$) is
the total amount it changes, namely $f(x+h) - f(x)$, divided by the amount the input changed, namely
$(x+h)-x = h$.
If you graph the function $f(x)$ on the $(x,y)$ plane, then geometrically
what you are doing is drawing a line between the points
$\bigl( x, f(x)\bigr)$ and $\bigl( x+h, f(x+h)\bigr)$ and calculating its {\it slope} (the ``rise over
run''). Such a line cutting through the function's graph is called a {\it secant line}, because
it {\it cuts} through the function, at the very least at those two aforementioned points.

By taking $h$ smaller and smaller (but never zero!) we continue to obtain secant lines, but the distance
between the two points $\bigl( x, f(x)\bigr)$ and $\bigl( x+h, f(x+h)\bigr)$ will get smaller and smaller.
You can imagine these points getting so close that it's as if the {\sl line touches the function exactly
once}, at the point $\bigl(x, f(x)\bigr)$ and nowhere else. This line, if it exists, will be called
the {\it tangent line}, but we're not ready to precisely define it just yet.

All we note at this point is that if we admit the concept of the ``instantaneous rate of change,'' and
allow that it can be approximated by taking the the average rate of change over smaller and smaller
intervals, then the slope of this tangent line (if it exists!) is exactly the instantaneous rate of change
that we seek. So it seems that the slope of the tangent line should be the value of the quotient
$$ {f(x+h) - f(x) \over (x+h) - x} = {f(x+h) - f(x) \over h}$$
when $h=0$, if only we could plug in $h=0$. But we cannot, since division by zero is undefined.

What we have learned from this preliminary section is that to understand rates of change,
we shall have to develop a precise concept of what it means to take a function
close to some input at which it is not necessarily defined. This is the notion of a {\it limit}.

\advsect Limits

\datestamp{02}{ix}
In this section we establish the notion of a limit, which will be a rigorous way of formalising
the idea of a function approaching a value at some point where it may or may not be defined.
To understand the definition of a limit, it might be helpful to think about it as a generalisation of the concept
of a continuous function $f$ equalling some value $y$ at the input $f(x)$.

Consider the very simple function $f(x) = x^2$. We know $f(3) = 3^2 = 9$. But since the function is continuous,
it is also the case that if you want the output to be close to $9$, then all you have to do is get the input
close to $3$. This is true in a {\it quantitative} sense!

If you want to get $f(x)$ to be within $0.1$ of $9$, then you just need to get $x$ within $0.01$ of the
input value $3$. How do we know this? Well $2.99^2 = 8.9401$ and $3.01^2 = 9.0601$, so (since the function
$x^2$ is nondecreasing for positive inputs) for any $x$
in the range $(2.99, 3.01)$, the value of $f(x)$ is in the range $(8.9, 9.1)$.

To sum up the previous paragraph in one sentence, you could say that in order for $f(x)$ to get
$0.1$-close to $9$, it suffices to pick any input $x$ that is $0.01$-close to $3$.
But since $f(3) = 9$ and $f$ is continuous, we can do much better. To guarantee that $f(x)$ be
$0.01$-close to $9$, we just need to ensure that $x$ is within $0.0001$ of $3$.
And in fact, for any small number $\eps>0$, there exists some $\delta$ such that whenever
$x\in (3-\delta, 3+\delta)$, we must have $f(x) \in (9-\eps, 9+\eps).$
(In math, we often use the Greek letters $\eps$ and $\delta$ to denote very small numbers.)

\medskip\boldlabel The quintessential non-example.
Here is an example to illustrate what we {\it don't} mean when we are talking about a function getting
arbitrarily close to a number when its input gets close to some number. Let
$$f(x) = \sin\Bigl({\pi\over x}\Bigr);$$
this function is defined everywhere except at $x=0$. The question is, does it ``approach'' anything
when $x=0$?
This function oscillates between $-1$ and $1$, but as $x$ approaches $0$ it oscillates very wildly.
You might be fooled into thinking that as $x$ approaches $0$, the function $f$ approaches $0$.
This is because
$$0 = \sin(\pi) = \sin(2\pi) = \sin(3\pi) = \cdots,$$
so
$f(1) = 0$, $f(1/2) = 0$, $f(1/3) = 0$, and so on.
But we also have
$$1 = \sin\Bigl({\pi\over 2}\Bigr) = \sin\Bigl({5\pi\over 2}\Bigr) = \sin\Bigl({9\pi\over 2}\Bigr) = \cdots,$$
giving us
$f(2) = 1$, $f(2/5) = 1$, $f(2/9) = 1$, etc., and so this is a case of $x$ approaching $0$ but
$f(x)$ {\it not} getting closer and closer to $0$.

So the notion we end up defining for $f$ getting close to something as $x$ approaches something should
not accidentally apply to this instance of $f$ above. We are just about ready to state this notion formally.

\medskip\boldlabel The formal definition of a limit.
First we define what a {\it neighbourhood} of a number
is; it is simply some (open) interval containing that number. For example, the interval $(2.5, 3.5)$
is a neighbourhood of the number $3$. So is $(2.9, 3.2)$, but $(10,100)$ is not, and neither is $(1.5, 3)$,
since we are considering open intervals.

Suppose that $f$ is defined on all inputs in some neighbourhood of the number $a$, except possibly at $a$ itself.
(In other words, suppose that there is some neighbourhood of $a$ contained in the domain of $f$, except
possibly the point $a$.)
We say that the the {\it limit} of the function $f$ at the input $a$ is equal to $L$, and write
$$\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L,$$
if for every $\eps > 0$, there exists some $\delta > 0$ such that whenever $x\ne a$ and
$x\in (a-\delta, a+\delta)$, we have $f(x) \in (L-\eps, L+\eps)$.

(If the use of the ``element of'' symbol $\in$
disturbs you,
you can write the latter two expressions as $a-\delta < x < a+\delta$ and $L-\eps < f(x) < L+\eps$,
respectively. Another way to write the definition of a limit is: For every $\eps >0$ there is some
$\delta >0$ such that whenever $0< |x-a| < \delta$, we have
$\bigl| f(x) - L\bigr| < \eps$. Convince yourself that this means the exact same thing mathematically as
the definition in terms of intervals that we had above---where
is the condition $x\ne a$ encoded in the second definition?)

Because the formal definition of a limit is rather cumbersome, involving parameters $\eps$ and $\delta$,
in many cases we can make do with the following ``simplified'' or ``intuitive'' definition of a limit.
{\sl
The limit of a function $f$ at the input $a$ is $L$ if we can make $f(x)$ as close as we like to $L$ by
requiring that $x$ be sufficiently close (but not necessarily equal to) $a$.}

In the example at the beginning of this section, we were convincing ourselves that
$$\lim_{x\to 3} x^2 = 9.$$
This should not be a mindblowing fact, since we of course have $3^2 = 9$. But we were careful in defining
the concept of a limit in such a way that it now applies to many more situations as well, not just when
$f(a)$ equals $L$.

\medskip\boldlabel A slightly harder example.
Let us now show an example of a function $f$ and an input $a$ such that $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)$ exists and equals
some number $L$, even when $f$ is not defined at the input $a$ itself. Suppose we let
$$f(x) = {3x^2-2x-1 \over x-1}.$$
Then we see that $f(x)$ is not defined at the input $x=1$, since $1-1 = 0$ and dividing by $0$ is
illegal. Nevertheless, if you graph this function $f$ using a graphing calculator or something like
Desmos, you will see that the function $f$ appears very simple. It is defined everywhere except at
$x=1$, and besides that little gap, its graph is simply a line; it is the unique line passing through
the points $(0,1)$ and $(2,7)$. So even though $f$ is not defined when $x=1$, we might suspect from this
picture that
$\lim_{x\to 1} f(x) = 4$.
Let us state this as a proposition and prove it rigorously (via the formal definition of a limit).

\edef\proplimfirstexample{\the\thmcount}
\proclaim Proposition \advthm. We have
$$\lim_{x\to 1} {3x^2-2x-1 \over x-1} = 4.$$

\proof Let $f(x) = (3x^2 - 2x-1)/(x-1)$ again for short. Suppose that $\eps > 0$ is given to us;
we need to prescribe some $\delta>0$ such that whenever $0<|x-1|<\delta$, we have
$0< \bigl| f(x) - 4\bigr| < \eps$. We shall show that picking $\delta = \eps/3$ works.

Here's why. Assume that $0<|x-1|<\delta = \eps/3$. Multiplying the second inequality by $3$ gives us
$$|3x-3| < \eps.$$
We want a term of $-4$ inside the absolute-value bars, so let's just add and subtract $1$ in that expression
to make that happen:
\edef\eqlimitexample{\the\eqcount}
$$|3x+1 - 4| < \eps\adveq$$
Now we're almost done---we would be done if we had $f(x)$ instead of $3x+1$. But note that
$$3x^2 - 2x-1 = (3x+1)(x-1),$$
so it's time to use the other assumption we had,
which was $|x-1| > 0$, to see that $(x-1)/(x-1) = 1$. In other words, under the assumption $|x-1|>0$,
we have
$$ 3x+1 = (3x+1)\cdot 1 = {(3x+1)(x-1)\over x-1} = {3x^2-2x-1\over x-1} = f(x).$$
Substituting this into~\refeq{\eqlimitexample} gives us
$0< \bigl| f(x) - 4\bigr| < \eps$,
which is precisely what we wanted to show.\slug

Picking $\delta = \eps/3$ seemed to require a great deal of algebraic ingenuity here; let's try to convince
ourselves that it's not so mysterious a choice.
If you draw the graph of $f$, you'll note that the slope near $x=1$ is $3$
(well it's actually $3$ everywhere!). So to guarantee no more than $\eps$ error in the output, we need
to be three times as precise in the input: we can have no more than $\eps/3$ error in the input.
This leads to the choice $\delta = \eps/3$. Note that the proof would also
work if we picked $\delta$ even smaller, e.g., $\delta = \eps/10$. But $\delta = \eps/3$ is the
largest one can take
$\delta$ for the proof to work, and it is a common stylistic choice for a mathematician to pick a maximal value
such as this.

Before moving on, we note here that you may at some point encounter authors who
write ``$f(x)\to L$ as $x\to a$.'' This is just an alternative way of writing $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$.
We will try to stick with the $\lim_{x\to a}$ version in these notes, as that is the convention the textbook
uses, but there is a strong argument for the $f(x)\to L$ notation as well, as it makes it clear that
$f(x)$ is not required to actually equal $L$ at any stage, it just needs to get arbitrarily close to it.

\medskip\boldlabel \datestamp{04}{ix} Formal negation of limit definition.
To fully understand the meaning of $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)$, it may be enlightening for us to
consider the negation of this statement. Concretely, what does it mean
for a number $L$ {\it not} to be the limit of a function $f$ at some input $a$?
Negating long mathematical statements (such as the definition of limit)
properly is not an easy task, and the untrained mathematician is liable to make errors especially concerning
quantifiers such as ``for all'' and ``there exists.''

The correct formal negation is the following: {\sl The real number $L$ is {\it not} a
limit of $f$ at the input $a$ if there exists some $\eps>0$ such that for all $\delta > 0$, there is
some $x$ in the domain of $f$ satisfying $0<|x-a|<\delta$ with the property that
$\bigl| f(x) - L\bigr| \ge \eps$.}

This is a fairly convoluted statement, but if you ponder it for a while you will convince yourself that
it does mean the opposite of what the limit definition says. When we write $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$, we mean
that you can get the values of $f(x)$ arbitrarily close to $L$ by taking $x$ sufficiently close to $a$,
and the opposite of this statement is there is some fixed positive distance $\eps$ such that even if you take
$x$ arbitrarily close to $a$, there will always be some badly-behaved choices of $x$ that result in
$f(x)$ being too far (at a distance at least $\eps$) from $f(x)$.

(Note that the negation of the mathematical statement $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$ should not be recorded as
$\lim_{x\to a} f(x)\ne L$, since even writing down the left-hand side of this inequality suggests that it
exists (is a real number), and as we shall see later in this section,
the limit of a function $f$ need not always exist at some given $a$.)

Here's an example to illustrate how you disprove the claim that the limit of a function $f$ at some $a$
is $L$.

\edef\proplimfnotzero{\the\thmcount}
\proclaim Proposition \advthm. Let
$$f(x) = \cases{-1, & if $x<0$; \cr 1, & if $x> 0$}.$$
Then the limit of $f$ at $0$ is not $0$.

\proof We just need to find some $\eps>0$ such that for any choice of $\delta > 0$, there will always
be some real number $x$ with $0<|x-0|<\delta$ but $\bigl| f(x) - 0 \bigr| \ge \eps$. It turns out that
choosing $\eps = 1/2$ works.

To see this, let $\delta>0$ be given. We need to pick some $x$ with $|x-0|<\delta$ but with
$\bigl| f(x) -0\bigr| \ge 1/2$. This is not so hard---we have lots of choices! Choosing $x = \delta/2$ works,
since $f(x) = f(\delta/2) = 1$, and $|1| \ge 1/2$.

Hence the limit of $f$ at $0$ is not $0$.\slug

\medskip\boldlabel \llap{*}A game with the Adversary.
Let $f$ be a function, let $L$ be a number, and let $a$ be a number such that $f$ is defined in some
neighbourhood of $a$.
The statement ``$\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$'' is either true or
false (the ``law of the excluded middle'' tells us that every mathematical statement is either true or false).
We can interpret its truthhood or falsity as the result of a game played between ourselves and a
supernatural Adversary. Here's the how the game goes.
\algbegin Game L (The limit game). Let $f$ be a function, $L$ a number, and $a$ a number with a neighbourhood
contained in the domain of $f$.
\algstep L1. [Adversary's turn.] The Adversary is allowed to pick any $\eps > 0$.
\algstep L2. [Our turn.] We can then pick any $\delta > 0$.
\algstep L3. [Adversary's turn.] The Adversary is then
allowed to pick any $x$ in the domain of $f$ that satisfies
$$0<|x-a|<\delta.$$
(This means that $x$ is at distance less than $\delta$ from $a$, but doesn't equal $a$.)
\algstep L4. [Verdict?] If
$$\bigl| f(x) - L\bigr| < \eps,$$
that is, if the distance between $f(x)$ and $L$ is less than $\eps$,
then we win; otherwise the Adversary wins.\slug

If $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)= L$ is true, then we can always win; the very definition of limit tells us
that no matter what $\eps$ is chosen in step L1 by the Adversary,
in step L2 there is always a choice of $\delta$ that will force the Adversary into letting us win in step
L3.
On the other hand, if $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$ is false (either because
this limit does not exist or because it equals
something else), then the Adversary can always win. There is a choice of $\eps$ in step L1 such that
no matter what we pick in step L2, the Adversary can beat us by making a further nefarious choice for $x$
in step L3.

\medskip\boldlabel Nonexistent limits.
We alluded earlier to the possibility of a function $f$ not having any limits at some point $a$.
We say that the limit of $f$ at $a$ {\it does not exist} if
for all real numbers $L$, the limit of $f$ at $a$ is not $L$. In fact, the function $f$ from
the previous proposition has this property at $a=0$.
The proof is largely unchanged from the easier case above when we had just set $L$ to $0$, requiring only
a little more care in the second paragraph.

\edef\proplimdne{\the\thmcount}
\proclaim Proposition \advthm. For $x\ne 0$, let
$$f(x) = {|x|\over x}.$$
Then the limit of $f$ as $x$ approaches $0$ does not exist.

\proof
We can re-express $f$ by writing
$$f(x) = \cases{-1, & if $x<0$; \cr 1, & if $x> 0$},$$
which may be clearer to some readers (and also shows this is the same function from
Proposition~{\proplimfnotzero}).
Let $L$ be arbitrary. We need to show that the limit of $f$ at $0$ is not $L$.
To do this, we shall pick $\eps>0$ such that for any choice of $\delta > 0$, there is
some real number $x$ with $0<|x-0|<\delta$ but $\bigl| f(x) - L\bigr| \ge \eps$.
Just as in the previous proof for the special case $L=0$, the choice $\eps = 1/2$ works.

Let $\delta > 0$ be given. We need to pick some $x$ with $|x-0|<\delta$ but with
$\bigl| f(x) -L\bigr| \ge 1/2$. Here it matters whether $L$ is positive or negative (if $L=0$ then
just appeal to the proof of Proposition~{\proplimfnotzero}). If $L$ is positive,
then choose $x=-\delta/2$. We have $0<|-\delta/2 - 0| <\delta$ as required, and also
$$\bigl| f(-\delta/2) - L\bigr| = | -1 - L| >  | -1 - 0|
\ge 1/2 = \eps.$$
On the other hand, if $L$ is negative, we instead choose $x=\delta/2$, so we still
have $0<|\delta/2 - 0|<\delta$ as needed, and this time
$$\bigl| f(\delta/2) - L\bigr| = | 1 - L| >  | 1 - 0|
\ge 1/2 = \eps.$$
Either way, we have satisfied the criterion of the limit at $f$ at $a$ not being $L$.

But the previous paragraph works for all numbers $L$, so the limit of $f$ at $a$ cannot equal
any number! Hence we have proved that it does not exist.\slug

In the example just given, we now know that the limit of $f$ at $0$ does not exist.
However, we
often think of a limit intuitively as the function ``approaching'' some value, and in this sense it's wrong to
say that $f$ doesn't approach anything at all when the input gets close to $0$. When you approach from
the right, it is equal to $1$. And when you approach from
the left, the function equals $-1$. This leads us to the concept of a one-sided limit.

\medskip\boldlabel One-sided limits.
Let $f$ be a function, let $L$ be a number, and let $a$ be a number such that $f$ is defined to the right of $a$.
(More precisely, we assume that $f$ is defined on the interval $(a,a+\eta)$ for some $\eta>0$.)
We say that {\it limit of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $a$ from the right is $L$}, and write
$$\lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) = L,$$
if for all $\eps>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that any number $x$ in the domain of $f$ with
$$a < x < a+\delta$$
must satisfy
$$ \bigl| f(x) - L\bigr| < \eps.$$
We might say in this case that $L$ is the {\it right-hand limit} of $f$.

Likewise, let $f$ be a function, $L$ be a number, and suppose that $a$ is such that $f$ is defined
to the {\it left} of $a$.
We say that {\it limit of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $a$ from the left is $L$}, and write
$$\lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) = L,$$
if for all $\eps>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that any number $x$ in the domain of $f$ with
$$a-\delta < x < a$$
must satisfy
$$ \bigl| f(x) - L\bigr| < \eps.$$
If this is true then we say $L$ is the {\it left-hand limit} of $f$.

More loosely, $L$ is the right-hand limit of $f$ at $a$ if you can get $f(x)$ to be arbitrarily close to $L$ by
requiring that $x$ be sufficiently close to $a$ {\it and greater than $a$},
and it's the left-hand limit of $f$ at $a$ if you can get $f(x)$ as close as you like to $L$
by requiring that $x$ be sufficiently close to $a$ {\it and smaller than $a$}.

Both of these definitions are relaxations of the definition of limit; it is ``easier'' to be a left-hand
limit than it is to be a {\it bona fide} limit). For example, we saw in Proposition~{\proplimdne}
that $1$ is not a limit of $f$ from that proposition, because $f$ has no limits.
However, once you have digested the definitions of one-sided limits, you should be able to
check that $1$ is the right-hand limit of $f$ at $0$, and $-1$ is the left-hand limit at $0$.

If the actual limit of $f$ at a point $a$ is $L$, then it is a consequence of the definitions that
the right-hand limit and the left hand limit are also $L$. Conversely, if the one-sided limits both equal
the same $L$, then the limit is actually $L$. This can be summed up symbolically in the following statement,
which we'll call a theorem since it comes in handy now and again.

\edef\thmonesidedsame{\the\thmcount}
\proclaim Theorem \advthm. For any function $f$ and numbers $a$ and $L$, we have
$$\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$$
if and only if
$$\lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) = L\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad \lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) = L.\noskipslug$$

A simple application of Theorem~{\thmonesidedsame} is to prove that the function $f(x) = |x|$ goes to $0$ as $x$
approaches $0$. We'll see this as an example in the next section, but it's really not a difficult proof,
so you could try it now. Show that both one-sided limits approach $0$, then simply invoke the theorem.

Here's a related theorem (no less useful) that tells us what happens if the one-sided limits
of a function do not agree at some given point. It shows that the example exhibited by Proposition~{\proplimdne}
is an instance of a very general phenomenon.

\edef\thmonesideddiff{\the\thmcount}
\proclaim Theorem \advthm. If
$$\lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) = L,\qquad \lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) = M,$$
and $L\ne M$, then $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)$ does not exist.

\proof We skip this proof as it's beyond the scope of this course (you would see it in, e.g., {\mc MATH} 242
or {\mc MATH} 254), but it is not so different in spirit
to the proof of Proposition~{\proplimdne}. The motivated student is encouraged to try it as an
advanced exercise. (If you try for a while and get stuck, email me or come to office hours with what
you have and I could help you fill in the details.)\slug

If we were told Theorem~{\thmonesideddiff} from the start, we could have used it to prove
Proposition~{\proplimdne} without much ado.

\medskip\noindent{\it Proof of Proposition~{\proplimdne} using Theorem~{\thmonesideddiff}.}\enspace
Recall that we are trying to prove that the limit of the function $f(x) = |x|/x$ does not exist
as $x$ approaches $0$.
Since $|x|  = x$ for all $x>0$, we see that
$$\lim_{x\to 0^+} {|x|\over x} = \lim_{x\to 0^+} {x\over x} = \lim_{x\to 0^+} 1 = 1.$$
(The last equality is easy to see if you just graph the constant function $1$. If you are a serious
student and insist on a rigorous proof, then please do Exercise~24 of Section~2.4 in the textbook,
which invites you to furnish
a proof that $\lim_{x\to a} c = c$ for all numbers $a$ and $c$.)

On the other hand, since $|x| = -x$ for all $x<0$, we have
$$\lim_{x\to 0^-} {|x|\over x} = \lim_{x\to 0^-} {-x\over x} = \lim_{x\to 0^+} -1 = -1.$$
So the left- and right-hand limits do not agree, and Theorem~{\thmonesideddiff} applies in
this situation to tell us that the limit does not exist.\slug

One-sided limits not agreeing is one way that the limit of a function at a given point can fail to exist.
Another way is for the limit to get arbitrarily large, which is the situation we tackle next.

\medskip\boldlabel \datestamp{09}{ix} Infinite limits and vertical asymptotes.
We say that the {\it limit of $f(x)$ is infinity as $x$ approaches $a$ from the right}, and write
$$\lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) = \infty,$$
if for every large (positive) number $M$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that every $x$ satisfying
$$a < x < a+\delta$$
has
$$f(x) > M.$$
If, instead, for every large positive number $M$ there is $\delta > 0$ such that every $x$ with
$$a < x < a+\delta$$
satisfies
$$f(x) < -M,$$
then we say that the limit of $f(x)$ is {\it minus infinity} as $x$ approaches $a$ from the right,
and write
$$\lim_{x\to a-} f(x) = -\infty.$$
The same definition can be made for left-hand limits by replacing the assumption $a<x<a+\delta$
with the assumption $a-\delta<x<a$ above.
This makes for four situations in total, and if any one of these situations occurs, then $f$ is
said to have a {\it vertical asymptote} at the line $x=a$.

Just as in the case of finite limits, when the left- and right-hand limits agree (and are both
the same type of infinity), then we simply say that the {\it limit} is infinity
(or minus infinity, accordingly). In other words,
we have
$$\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = \infty$$
if and only if
$$\lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) = \infty\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad \lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) = \infty,$$
and we have
$$\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = -\infty$$
if and only if
$$\lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) = -\infty\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad \lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) = -\infty.$$

(Let $p$ and $q$ be mathematical statements. The phrase ``$p$ if $q$'' means that $q$ implies $p$,
and the phrase ``$p$ only if $q$'' means that $p$ implies $q$---think about it for a second, it makes sense!
So the statement ``$p$ if and only if $q$'' means that $p$ is true exactly when $q$ is true, and vice versa.)

One should not interpret the symbol $\infty$ above as a number, since there is no such number.
When we write $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = \infty$,
we simply mean that this expression can be made arbitrarily large as by requiring that $x$ be sufficiently
close to $a$. It should also be stressed that the equality symbol in this expression should {\it not}
be interpreted
as meaning that the limit of $f$ at $a$ exists, because the terminology of a limit ``existing'' is reserved
for when the limit equals a number (and, once again, $\infty$ is not a number). As with finite limits,
sometimes authors write, e.g., $f(x)\to -\infty$ as $x\to a$ instead of $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = -\infty$.
This notation is also valid, and in some sense preferable as it does not suggest that $f(x)$ ever ``equals''
some ``number'' $\infty$.

Let's look at a couple of simple examples of functions with vertical asymptotes.

\edef\propvertasymp{\the\thmcount}
\proclaim Proposition \advthm. We have
$$\lim_{x\to 0^+} {1\over x} = \infty$$
and
$$\lim_{x\to 0^-} {1\over x} = -\infty.$$

\proof We prove both statements separately. The first asserts that $1/x$ can be made
arbitrarily large by taking $x\to 0$ from the right. To prove it, let $M>0$ be an arbitrarily large number
(given to us). Pick $\delta = 1/M$, and assume that $0<x<\delta = 1/M$. Because $x>0$, we are allowed to
write $1/x$, and the fact that $x < 1/M$ implies that $1/x > M$, which is what we needed to prove
in the definition of infinite limit.

The second statement is proved extremely similarly, but we include the full proof for completeness.
Let $M$ be given, and pick $\delta = 1/M$. Assume that $-\delta < x<0$; the right inequality here
assures us that the expression $1/x$ is well-defined, and the left inequality here can be rewritten as
$x > -\delta = -1/M$. Rearranging the equation now yields $1/x < -M$, which is what we needed to show
in order to prove that the limit is $-\infty$.\slug

To sum up this proof, we can make $1/x$ arbitrarily large to the right of $0$ by requiring that $x>0$
be sufficiently close to $0$, and we can make $1/x$ an arbitrarily large negative number by requiring
that $x<0$ be sufficiently close to (the left of) $0$.

Here's another example where the left- and right-hand limits agree on the type of infinity.

\proclaim Proposition \advthm. The limit of
$$f(x) = {1\over (x-3)^2}$$
is infinity as $x$ approaches $3$.

\proof We can do the left- and right-hand limits separately, but it is not hard to prove
this whole thing all at once (i.e., to consider both sides of $3$ at the same time).

Let $M>0$ be given. We want to prescribe $\delta > 0$ such that all $x\ne 3$ with
$$3-\delta < x < 3+\delta$$
satisfy
$${1\over (x-3)^2} > M.$$
(The assumption $3-\delta < x < 3+\delta$ is what we mean by considering both sides of $3$ at the same time;
note that this assumption together with the assumption that $x\ne 3$ can be summed up in the single inequality
$0<|x-3|<\delta$.)
Setting $\delta = 1/\sqrt M$ works this time, so our assumption on $x$ will be
$$3-{1\over \sqrt M} < x < 3+{1\over \sqrt M},$$
and that $x\ne 3$ of course, so that $f(x)$ is even defined.
The difference $x-3$ may be positive or negative; we don't know which. But what we do know, from our assumption,
is that the distance $|x-3|$ between $x$ and $3$ satisfies
$$|x-3| < {1\over \sqrt M}.$$
Squaring both sides (and using the fact that $|x-3|^2 = (x-3)^2$, since the square of a negative number is
positive anyway) gives us
$$(x-3)^2 < {1\over M},$$
and rearranging yields
$$f(x) = {1\over (x-3)^2} > M,$$
which is what we needed to show. (The number $M$ was arbitrary in this proof, so we can keep repeating
with larger and larger $M$ to show that $f(x)$ can be made arbitrarily large in the way shown above.)\slug

Once again, picking $\delta = 1/\sqrt M$ made the algebra work out nicely, but you could have written
a valid proof by picking, say, $\delta = 1/M$ instead, but then there'd be some issues with having to
assume that $M>1$. (Morally this
is {\mc O.K.}, since we're only worried about what happens when $M$ is very large, but {\it technically}
our definition had $M>0$.)
Don't stress too much about picking the ``best''
value for $\delta$. Generally, you find $\delta$ by first trying out various values of $M$ and seeing what
$\delta$ is necessary to deal with that $M$; after a few tries you should be able to come up with a formula
that works generally. The broader thing to take away from reading this proof or trying to do it yourself
is that no matter what $M$ is given, you can
pick {\it some} $\delta$ that makes the proof work out.


\medskip\boldlabel \datestamp{11}{ix} Limits at infinity and horizontal asymptotes.
There is one last type of limit that remains to be defined. This is the case in which the values of
$f(x)$ can be taken arbitrarily close to a value by making $x$ sufficiently large, either in the positive
direction or the negative direction. Formally, we write
$$\lim_{x\to \infty} f(x) = L$$
and say that the {\it limit of $f(x)$ is $L$ as $x$ approaches infinity} if for all $\eps > 0$
there exists some large $M>0$ such that every $x$ with $x>M$ has $\bigl| f(x) - L\bigr| < \eps$.
Likewise, we say the {\it limit of $f(x)$ is $L$ as $x$ approaches negative infinity} and write
$$\lim_{x\to -\infty} f(x) = L$$
if the same condition applies, except the inequality $x>M$ is replaced by $x<-M$.
In either of these situations, we say that $y=f(x)$ has a {\it horizontal asymptote} at the line
$y=L$.

Note that the same example from Proposition~{\propvertasymp} also exhibits a horizontal asymptote.

\edef\prophorasymp{\the\thmcount}
\proclaim Proposition \advthm. We have
$$\lim_{x\to \infty} 1/x = 0\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad \lim_{x\to-\infty} 1/x = 0.$$

\proof Let $\eps >0$ and pick $M = 1/\eps$. Then any number $x$ with $x>M$ has
$$\biggl|{1\over x} - 0\,\biggr| = {1\over x} < {1\over M} = \eps,$$
so $\lim_{x\to \infty} 1/x = 0$.

For the other inequality, the proof is very similar. Let $\eps > 0$ and again we choose $M = 1/\eps$.
Now any (negative) number $x$ with $x < -M$ has
$$\biggl|{1\over x} - 0\,\biggr| = \biggl|{1\over x}\biggr| = {1\over -x} < {1\over M} = \eps,$$
so $\lim_{x\to -\infty} 1/x = 0$.\slug

Since $\infty$ is not a number, it does not make sense to write an expression such as, say, $1/\infty$.
On the other hand, you may have heard people say, informally, something like ``anything divided by infinity
is zero.'' This statement can be made into a proper mathematical one by expressing it in terms of limits.
It is true that for any fixed number $K$, the limit $\lim_{x\to \infty} K/x$ equals $0$. (As an exercise,
prove this directly by very slightly modifying the proof of Proposition~{\prophorasymp} above.
Hint: The proposition already gives a proof when $K=1$. So for the general case, instead of setting
$M = 1/\eps$, you might try choosing something very similar but involving $K$.)

\medskip\boldlabel Infinite limits at infinity.
As with limits as $x$ approaches some finite $a$, the infinite limit is said not to exist if it does
not equal any finite number $L$. There is one special case of this worth mentioning, which is when
the function $f(x)$ can be made arbitrarily large (positive or negative) by requiring that $x$
be sufficiently large (positive or negative). Hence there are four cases of infinite limits at infinity,
namely
$$\lim_{x\to \infty} f(x) = \infty,\qquad
\lim_{x\to -\infty} f(x) = \infty,$$
$$\lim_{x\to \infty} f(x) = -\infty,\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad
\lim_{x\to -\infty} f(x) = -\infty.$$
Tedious as it is, let us formally define all four of these statements, for the sake of completeness.
\medskip
\item{a)}
We say that {\it $f(x)$ goes to infinity as $x$ goes to infinity} and write
$$\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) = \infty$$
if for all $M>0$, there exists $N>0$ such
that for all $x$ with $x > N$, we have $f(x) > M$.
\smallskip
\item{b)}
We say that {\it $f(x)$ goes to infinity as $x$ goes to negative infinity} and
write
$$\lim_{x\to-\infty} f(x) = \infty$$
if for all $M>0$, there exists $N>0$ such
that for all $x$ with $x < -N$, we have $f(x) > M$.
\smallskip
\item{c)}
We say that {\it $f(x)$ goes to negative infinity as $x$ goes to infinity} and
write
$$\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) = -\infty$$
if for all $M>0$, there exists $N>0$ such
that for all $x$ with $x > N$, we have $f(x) < -M$.
\smallskip
\item{d)}
We say that {\it $f(x)$ goes to negative infinity as $x$ goes to negative infinity} and
write
$$\lim_{x\to-\infty} f(x) = -\infty$$
if for all $M>0$, there exists $N>0$ such
that for all $x$ with $x < -N$, we have $f(x) < -M$.
\medskip\noindent
That was a mouthful. But by this point it is hoped that the reader would be comfortable enough with these
formal definitions to be able to formulate, say (a), (c), and (d) after being shown (b).

We finish off this very long section by presenting
an example of a function that seems to grow fairly slowly $x\to\infty$, but nevertheless still goes to
infinity as $x$ is taken to be large.

\proclaim Proposition \advthm. We have
$$\lim_{x\to\infty} \root 3\of x = \infty$$
and
$$\lim_{x\to-\infty} \root 3\of x = -\infty.$$

\proof Let $M>0$ be a large positive number given to us.
We want to pick $N$ such that for all $x>N$, we have $\root 3\of x = x^{1/3} > M$.
After a moment's pause we see that choosing $N = M^3$ works, since if we assume that $x>N$, then
$$x^{1/3} > N^{1/3} = (M^3)^{1/3} = M.$$

The negative infinite limit is proved similarly; we choose $N = M^3$ this time as well, so that
whenever $x<-N$, we have
$$x^{1/3} < (-N)^{1/3} = (-M^3)^{1/3} = -M.$$
This completes the proof.\slug

\advsect Rules for evaluating limits

In the previous section, we already encountered two rules (namely, Theorems~{\thmonesidedsame}
and~{\thmonesideddiff}) that can help us find limits of functions
or determine that they do not exist. This section will be devoted to establishing a few more useful laws.
First we record a list of algebraic laws, which show that the limit behaves well under a number of algebraic
operations such as addition, multiplication, etc.

\edef\thmalglaws{\the\thmcount}
\parenproclaim Theorem {\advthm} (Algebraic limit laws).
Let $c$ be any fixed number, and suppose that $f$ and $g$ are functions such that
$$\lim_{x\to a} f(x)\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad \lim_{x\to a} g(x)$$
both exist. Then
\medskip
\item{i)} $\lim_{x\to a} \bigl( f(x) + g(x)\bigr) = \lim_{x\to a} f(x) + \lim_{x\to a} g(x)$;
\smallskip
\item{ii)} $\lim_{x\to a} \bigl( f(x) - g(x)\bigr) = \lim_{x\to a} f(x) - \lim_{x\to a} g(x)$;
\smallskip
\item{iii)} $\lim_{x\to a} cf(x) = c\cdot \lim_{x\to a} f(x)$;
\smallskip
\item{iv)} $\lim_{x\to a} \bigl( f(x)g(x)\bigr) = \lim_{x\to a} f(x) \cdot \lim_{x\to a} g(x)$;
\smallskip
\item{v)} $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)/g(x) = \lim_{x\to a} f(x) \bigl/ \lim_{x\to a} g(x)$, provided
that $\lim_{x\to a} g(x)\ne 0$;
\smallskip
\item{vi)} for all positive integers $n$, $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)^n = \bigl(\lim_{x\to a} f(x)\bigr)^n$; and
\smallskip
\item{vii)} for all positive integers $n$, $\lim_{x\to a} \root n\of{f(x)} = \root n\of{\lim_{x\to a} f(x)}$,
(provided that $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)>0$ in the case that $n$ is even ,so that the $n$th root is well-defined).
\medskip
These statements are all true even when $x\to a$ is replaced with $x\to\infty$, $x\to-\infty$,
$x\to a^+$, or $x\to a^-$.

The identities above
are known as (i) the sum law, (ii) the difference law, (iii) the constant multiple law,
(iv) the product law, (v) the quotient law, (vi) the power law, and (vii) the root law, respectively.
They can be expressed in words as well:~the limit of a sum is the sum of the limits,
the limit of a difference is the difference
of the limits, and so on.

\medskip\noindent{\it \datestamp{14}{ix} Proof.}\xskip\ignorespaces
We only prove (i) in these notes.
Laws (ii) through (v) are proved in Appendix~F of the textbook. Law (vi) is a consequence of repeating
law (iv) $n$ times (formally, you would need to use the {\it principle of mathematical induction},
which is outside the scope of this course), and the proof of law (vii) is assigned to you in the textbook
as Exercise~69 of Section~2.5.
All of these proofs only deal with the case that $x\to a$ for some finite number $a$,
as opposed to $x\to\infty$; it is an instructive exercise to try and adapt the proof below for the
$x\to \infty$ case, to test that you have fully digested this proof and the definition of limits at infinity.

Enough talk. On to the proof of (i). We shall make use of the
{\it triangle inequality}, which says that for any numbers $a$, $b$, and $c$, we have
$$|a-c| \le |a-b| + |b-c|.$$
In other words, the length of any side in a triangle is always smaller than the sum of the other two sides.

Suppose $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L_1$ and $\lim_{x\to a} g(x) = L_2$. We want to show that the limit
of $f(x) + g(x)$ as $x\to a$ is $L_1 + L_2$.
Let $\eps>0$. We wish to pick a $\delta>0$ such that for all $x$ with $0<|x-a|<\delta$, we have
$$\bigl| f(x) + g(x) - (L_1 + L_2) \bigr| <\eps.$$
Well, since $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L_1$, we can make $f(x)$ arbitrarily close to $L_1$ by prescribing
that $x$ be sufficiently close to $a$. So there is some $\delta_1 > 0$ such that if we assume
$0<|x-a|<\delta_1$, then
$$\bigl| f(x) - L_1\bigr| < {\eps\over 2}.$$
(We choose $\eps/2$ so that there is some additional room for error when we deal with $g$.)
Likewise, there is some $\delta_2>0$ such that if we assume
$0<|x-a|<\delta_2$, then
$$\bigl| g(x) - L_2\bigr| < {\eps\over 2}.$$

Now, set $\delta = \min\{\delta_1, \delta_2\}$. This guarantees both $\delta\le \delta_1$ and
$\delta\le \delta_2$, so that if we assume $0<|x-a|<\delta$,
then the inequalities
$$0<|x-a|<\delta_1\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad 0<|x-a|<\delta_2$$
{\it both} hold. As a result, by our observations in the previous paragraph,
both of the inequalities
$$\bigl| f(x) - L_1\bigr| < {\eps\over 2}
\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad \bigl| g(x) - L_2\bigr| < {\eps\over 2}$$
are true as well.
Finally we see that
$$\eqalign{
\bigl| f(x) + g(x) - (L_1 + L_2) \bigr| &= \bigl| f(x) - L_1 + g(x) - L_2\bigr| \cr
&\le \bigl| f(x) - L_1\bigr| + \bigl| g(x) - L_2\bigr| \cr
&\le {\eps\over 2} + {\eps \over 2} \cr
&= \eps, \cr
}$$
where the second line follows from the aforementioned triangle inequality. This completes the proof.\slug

These laws are extremely powerful in general, allowing us to express the limit of a complex expression
in terms of the limits of simpler expressions. For example, using laws (i), (iii), and (iv), we can deduce that
\edef\eqlimpoly{\the\eqcount}
$$\lim_{x\to 3} 4x^2-7 = 4\cdot \Bigl( \lim_{x\to 3} x\Bigr) \Bigl( \lim_{x\to 3} x\Bigr) -
\lim_{x\to 3} 7.\adveq$$
We already encountered
an expression of the form $\lim_{x\to a} c$, where $c$ is a constant number, in an earlier proof.
It should be obvious that this limit is $c$, and that $\lim_{x\to a} x = a$. Let us state these two facts,
along with two more as a theorem, so that we can easily invoke them later on. (We continue the numbering
from Theorem~{\thmalglaws} in order to remain consistent with the textbook.)

\edef\thmspeciallimits{\the\thmcount}
\parenproclaim Theorem {\advthm} (Special limits). For any positive integer $n$ and any numbers $a$ and $c$,
\medskip
\begingroup\parindent=22pt
\item{viii)} $\lim_{x\to a} c = c$;
\smallskip
\item{ix)} $\lim_{x\to a} x = a$;
\smallskip
\item{x)} $\lim_{x\to a} x^n = a^n$; and
\smallskip
\item{xi)} $\lim_{x\to a} \root n\of x = \root n\of a$.
\endgroup
\medskip
Laws (viii) and (ix) remain true when $x\to a$ is replaced by $x\to\infty$ or $x\to-\infty$.

\proof As mentioned before, the proof of law (viii) is presented to you as Exercise~24 in Section~2.4;
the proof of law (ix) is Exercise~23 in the same section. This is in the case where $x$ approaches some
finite $a$; you might like to prove the corresponding
limits at infinity to test your understanding of those definitions.

Laws (x) and (xi) are easy to prove once we have
all the other laws established.
To prove law (x), apply the power law with $f(x) = x$ and then law (ix) to obtain
$$\lim_{x\to a} x^n = \Bigl( \lim_{x\to a} x\Bigr)^n = a^n.$$
To prove law (xi), apply the root law with $f(x) = x$ and then law (ix) to obtain
$$\lim_{x\to a} \root n\of x = \root n\of{ \lim_{x\to a} x} = \root n\of a.\noskipslug$$


With these new laws in hand, we can revisit the polynomial $4x^2-7$ from earlier and conclude that
$$\lim_{x\to 3} (4x^2 - 7) = 4\cdot 3^2 - 7 = 29.$$
In fact, we don't even need to break up $x^2$ into $x\cdot x$ like we did
in \refeq{\eqlimpoly}, as law (x) takes care of monomials
such as these.

When applying the limit laws to a determine what happens when $x\to 3$ to a
simple polynomial such as $4x^2-7$, it seemed like
we could just substitute in $x=3$ in the expression and get the right answer. This turns out to be true in
the general case $x\to a$ as long as $a$ is in the domain of $f$.

\edef\thmdirectsub{\the\thmcount}
\parenproclaim Theorem {\advthm} (Direct substitution).
Let $f$ be a polynomial or a rational function (a quotient of two polynomials). If $a$ is in the domain of
$f$, then
$$\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = f(a).$$

\proof Every rational function $f$ is of the form
$$f(x) = {a_m x^m + a_{m-1} x^{m-1} + \cdots + a_1 x + a_0
\over b_n x^n + b_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \cdots + b_1 x + b_0},$$
where $m$ and $n$ are nonnegative integers, and $a_0,a_1,\ldots,a_m,b_0,b_1,\ldots,b_n$
are real (possibly negative or zero) coefficients.
Taking the limit of both sides, the
quotient law deals with the fraction, the sum law splits the limit of the sum into the sum of limits, and
from there we use law (x) and the constant multiple law to break up these terms further. We then apply
laws (viii) and (ix) as to finish off the proof.\slug

This theorem applies to functions involving roots as well, thanks to the root law. (It more generally
applies to all {\it continuous} functions---we will discuss the notion of continuity very soon.)
Using the direct substitution rule is fairly easy whenever its hypotheses are satisfied,
so we will not dwell on this.
There is, however, a more advanced version of this rule that is useful in certain cases, such as when
$f(x)$ is a rational
function and $a$ is {\it not} in the domain of $f$ (e.g., because plugging in $x=a$ makes the denominator $0$).
Then we may have to invoke the following theorem in order to use the direct substitution rule.

\edef\thmlimequal{\the\thmcount}
\proclaim Theorem \advthm. Let $a$ be a number, and suppose that $f$ and $g$ are two functions
that are equal for all $x$ in some interval containing $a$, except possibly at $a$ itself.
Then if $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)$ and $\lim_{x\to a} g(x)$ both exist, we must have
$$\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = \lim_{x\to a} g(x).$$

\optproof This proof is a bit more advanced than you should fairly be expected to follow in this course,
though if you take a second-year mathematics class such as {\mc MATH} 242 one day,
you'd find it a routine exercise by the end of that course.
We include it in these notes for completeness.

We know that $f$ and $g$ agree in some interval containing $a$, except possibly at $a$ itself.
To be concrete, this means there exists some number $\eta>0$ be such that $f(x) = g(x)$ for every
$x$ with $0<|x-a|<\eta$.
Suppose $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$ and $\lim_{x\to a} = M$. We want to show that $L=M$; we shall show
that $|L-M|<\eps$ for all $\eps > 0$, which is an equivalent statement (a nonnegative quantity that is
less than any positive quantity must be zero).

Let $\eps > 0$. We will use an ``$\eps/2$ trick'' again, just as in the proof of the sum law above.
Since $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$, there is $\delta_1$ such that for all $x$ with $0<|x-a|<\delta_1$,
we have
\edef\eqfxL{\the\eqcount}
$$\bigl| f(x) - L\bigr| < \eps/2.\adveq$$
Likewise, since $\lim_{x\to a} g(x) = M$, there is $\delta_2$
such that for all $x$ with $0<|x-a|<\delta_2$, we have
\edef\eqgxM{\the\eqcount}
$$\bigl| g(x) - M\bigr| < \eps/2.\adveq$$
Now pick $\delta = \min\{\eta, \delta_1, \delta_2\}$,
so that every $x$ with $0<|x-a|<\delta$ has $f(x) = g(x)$ {\it and} satisfies both~\refeq{\eqfxL}
and~\refeq{\eqgxM}. We now combine all three of these facts and invoke the triangle inequality to obtain
$$\eqalign{
|L-M| &= \bigl|L - g(x) + g(x) - M\bigr| \cr
&\le \bigl|L-g(x)\bigr| + \bigl|g(x) - M\bigr| \cr
&= \bigl| f(x) - L\bigr| + \bigl| g(x) - M\bigr| \cr
&< {\eps\over 2} + {\eps\over 2} \cr
&= \eps. \cr
}$$
As we said above, since this proof shows that $|L-M|<\eps$ for every $\eps > 0$, we must have $|L-M| = 0$;
that is, $L=M$.\slug

If we'd had these theorems from the start, we wouldn't have had to use so much elbow grease in
proving Proposition~{\proplimfirstexample}. Here's how we could have proceeded.

\medskip\noindent{\it Proof of Proposition~{\proplimfirstexample} using Theorems~{\thmdirectsub}
and~{\thmlimequal}.}\enspace
Let
$$f(x) = {3x^2 -2x-1\over x-1};$$
recall that we are trying to show that $\lim_{x\to 1} f(x) = 4$.
We factor $3x^2 - 2x-1 = (3x+1)(x-1)$ and observe that for any $x\ne 1$,
$$f(x) = {3x^2 -2x-1\over x-1} = 3x+1.$$
Letting $g(x) = 3x+1$, on any open interval containing $1$ we have $f(x) = g(x)$ everywhere except at
$x=1$, so Theorem~{\thmlimequal} applies to inform us that $\lim_{x\to 1} f(x) = \lim_{x\to 1} g(x)$.
Moreover, $g(x)$ is a polynomial with $x=1$ in its domain, so
$$\lim_{x\to 1} g(x) = 3\cdot 1 + 1 = 4$$
by Theorem~{\thmdirectsub}.\slug

Here's another example in which we use the root law.

\proclaim Proposition \advthm. We have
$$\lim_{x\to 0} {\sqrt{x^2+4}-2\over x^2} = {1\over 4}.$$

\proof
We cannot use the quotient law, since $x^2\to 0$ as $x\to 0$. If we can massage the expression to obtain
a factor of $x^2$ in the numerator, then (in an open interval containing $0$ but not at $0$ itself), we
can cancel the $x^2$ in the denominator and perhaps make some headway. This strategy is facilitated by
the observation that
$$ \Bigl(\sqrt{x^2 + 4} - 2\Bigr) \Bigl( \sqrt{x^2 - 4} + 2\Bigr) = \Bigl(\sqrt{x^2 - 4}\Bigr)^2 - 2^2
= x^2.$$
Hence we have
$$\eqalign{
\lim_{x\to 0} {\sqrt{x^2+4}-2\over x^2} &= \lim_{x\to 0} {\sqrt{x^2+4}-2\over x^2} \cdot {\sqrt{x^2 + 4} + 2
\over \sqrt{x^2 + 4} + 2} \cr
&=  \lim_{x\to 0} {x^2\over x^2 \bigl( \sqrt{x^2 + 4} + 2\bigr)} \cr
&= \lim_{x\to 0} {1\over \sqrt{x^2 + 4} + 2}. \cr
}$$
We no longer have the issue of the limit of the denominator being $0$, and can happily apply the algebraic
limit laws as well as the direct substitution property:
$$\eqalign{
\lim_{x\to 0} {1\over \sqrt{x^2 + 4} + 2}
&= {1\over \lim_{x\to 0} \bigl( \sqrt{x^2 + 4} + 2\bigr)} \cr
&= {1\over \lim_{x\to 0} \sqrt{x^2 + 4} + \lim_{x\to 0}  2} \cr
&= {1\over \sqrt{\lim_{x\to 0} (x^2 + 4)} +  2} \cr
&= {1\over \sqrt{4} +  2} \cr
&= {1\over 4} \noskipslug\cr
}$$

\medskip\boldlabel Using one-sided limits.
Recall Theorem~{\thmonesidedsame}, which states that a limit exists and equals some $L$ if and only if
both one-sided limits exist and equal $L$. This can be used to compute $\lim_{x\to 0} |x|$, even
though the algebraic limit laws do not apply to the function $|x|$ directly.

\edef\proplimabs{\the\thmcount}
\proclaim Proposition \advthm. We have
$$\lim_{x\to 0} |x| = 0.$$

\proof Towards the invocation of Theorem~{\thmonesidedsame}, we calculate the one-sided limits of
$|x|$ using the direct substitution rule.
When $x>0$, we have $|x| = x$, so
$$\lim_{x\to 0^+} |x| = \lim_{x\to 0^+} x = 0,$$
since
For all $x<0$ we instead have $|x| = -x$, so
$$\lim_{x\to 0^-} |x| = \lim_{x\to 0^-} -x = 0.$$
Both one-sided limits agree, so we conclude that the limit is $0$.\slug

\medskip\boldlabel Evaluating limits at infinity.
Recall that most of the algebraic limit laws established earlier in this section apply to limits at
infinity. Hence we can combine the root and power laws with Proposition~{\prophorasymp} to prove the following
useful theorem.

\edef\thminfratpower{\the\thmcount}
\proclaim Theorem \advthm. Let $r>0$ be a rational number. Then
$$\lim_{x\to \infty} {1\over x^r} = 0.$$
If $r>0$ is such that $x^r$ is defined for negative values of $x$ as well, then
$$\lim_{x\to -\infty} {1\over x^r} = 0.$$

\proof Let $r>0$ be a rational number. This means there are positive integers $m$ and $n$ such that
$r = m/n$, so we can write
$$\lim_{x\to \infty} {1\over x^r} = \lim_{x\to \infty} \root n\of{\Bigl({1\over x}\Bigr)^m}
=\root n\of{\Bigl(\lim_{x\to \infty} {1\over x} \Bigr)^m} = 0,$$
where the third equality follows from the root and power laws, and the last follows from
Proposition~{\prophorasymp}.

This works for $x\to-\infty$ as well, as long as $x^r$ is defined for negative values of $x$. (If
$n$ is even and $m$ is odd, $x^m$ is negative and the $n$th root of a negative is not defined,
so it does not makes sense to write $x^r = \root n\of {x^m}$ in this case.)\slug

When studying the behaviour of a function $f(x)$ as $x\to\infty$, we can always multiply the whole
expression by $x^r/x^r$ for any $r$, since we are interested in the case where $x$ is large and don't
have to worry about the denominator possibly being $0$. This is very useful in practice when applying
Theorem~{\thminfratpower}.

Here's a simple example.

\proclaim \datestamp{16}{ix} Proposition \advthm. We have
$$\lim_{x\to \infty} {5x^2 - 3x + 4\over 3x^2 + 10x-3} = {5\over 3}$$

\proof Since we're only interested in the behaviour when $x$ is very large, we can assume $x>0$, so that
$x^2/x^2$ is defined. We divide by this function, obtaining
$$\lim_{x\to \infty} {5x^2 - 3x + 4\over 3x^2 + 10x-3}
=\lim_{x\to \infty} {(5x^2 - 3x + 4)/x^2\over (3x^2 + 10x-3)/x^2}
=\lim_{x\to \infty} {5- 3/x + 4/x^2\over 3 + 10/x-3/x^2}.$$
Invoking the quotient and sum laws now, we take the limits of each term in the numerator and denominator
individually, and Theorem~{\thminfratpower} applies to give us
$$\lim_{x\to \infty} {5- 3/x + 4/x^2\over 3 + 10/x-3/x^2}
=\lim_{x\to \infty} {5-0 + 0\over 3 + 0-0} = {5\over 3}.$$
(We technically used the constant multiple law here too, e.g., when performing the operation
$\lim_{x\to\infty} 4/x^2 = 4\cdot\lim_{x\to\infty} 1/x^2$.)\slug

The limit as $x\to-\infty$ of the function in the above proposition is also $5/3$ (work it out!),
but it is not always the case that the limits as $x\to\infty$ and $x\to-\infty$ agree.

\proclaim Proposition \advthm. Let
$$f(x) = {\sqrt{2x^2+1} \over 5x-1}.$$
We have
\medskip
\item{i)} $\lim_{x\to \infty} f(x) = \sqrt 2/5$;
\smallskip
\item{ii)} $\lim_{x\to -\infty} f(x) = -\sqrt 2/5$;
\smallskip
\item{iii)} $\lim_{x\to (1/5)^+} f(x) = \infty$; and
\smallskip
\item{iv)} $\lim_{x\to (1/5)^-} f(x) = -\infty$.
\medskip

\proof To prove part (i), we perform the same strategy. The largest power of $x$ in the denominator is just
$x$, so we divide top and bottom by $x$, noting that for $x>0$ one has $x = \sqrt{x^2}$:
$$\eqalign{
\lim_{x\to\infty} {\bigl(\sqrt{2x^2+1}\bigr)/x \over (5x-1)/x}
&=\lim_{x\to\infty} {\bigl(\sqrt{2x^2+1}\bigr)/\sqrt{x^2} \over 5-1/x} \cr
&=\lim_{x\to\infty} {\bigl(\sqrt{2+1/x^2}\bigr) \over 5-1/x} \cr
&={\sqrt{2+0} \over 5-0} \cr
&= {\sqrt 2\over 5}\cr
}$$
It looks like we can do the same thing when $x<0$, and morally we can, except we have to remember that
for negative $x$ one instead has $x = -\sqrt{x^2}$. So
$$\eqalign{
\lim_{x\to-\infty} {\bigl(\sqrt{2x^2+1}\bigr)/x \over (5x-1)/x}
&=\lim_{x\to-\infty} {\bigl(\sqrt{2x^2+1}\bigr)/\bigl(-\sqrt{x^2}\bigr) \over 5-1/x} \cr
&=\lim_{x\to-\infty} {-\bigl(\sqrt{2+1/x^2}\bigr) \over 5-1/x} \cr
&={-\sqrt{2+0} \over 5-0} \cr
&= {-\sqrt 2\over 5},\cr
}$$
settling claim (ii).
We might as well take a moment here to practise finding infinite limits. The input $x=1/5$ is outside
the domain of $f$, since it makes the denominator $0$, so we should investigate the behaviour of this function
around this point. Note that when $x=1/5$ exactly, the numerator is a positive
number (since squaring a positive number preserves its positivity, and all the other operations in that
expression do as well---if you were extra insecure, you'd calculate the numerator fully and see that it
equals $\sqrt{27}/5$, a positive quantity). Hence slightly to the left of $x=1/5$ and slightly to the right,
$f$ is also positive. When $x$ just slightly to the right of $1/5$, the denominator is a very small positive
number, and we can make this denominator arbitrarily small by taking $x$ sufficiently close to $x$ from the
right. So
$$\lim_{x\to (1/5)^+} = \infty,$$
since the numerator is positive as well.
On the other hand, when $x$ is very close to $1/5$ from the left, the denominator is a very small negative
number, and again this can be taken arbitrarily close to $0$ by taking $x$ sufficiently close to $x$ from
the left. So
$$\lim_{x\to (1/5)^-} = -\infty.\noskipslug$$

The examples we've seen of limits at infinity have been finite, so before moving on,
let's see an example where the limit at infinity is infinite.

\proclaim Proposition \advthm. We have
$$\lim_{x\to\infty} x-x^2 = -\infty.$$

\proof We might be tempted to try the difference law here, but since the limits are both infinite we get
$\infty-\infty$, which is nonsense. A better thing to do is to factor $x-x^2 = x(1-x)$. When $x$ gets
large, $x$ is a large positive number and $(1-x)$ is a large negative number. Hence the product
is a (very) large negative number. We conclude that the limit must be $-\infty$.\slug

\medskip\boldlabel The squeeze theorem.
We end this section by highlighting the following intuitive theorem, which also goes by the names
{\it sandwich theorem} or {\it two policemen and a drunk theorem}.

\parenproclaim Theorem {\advthm} (Squeeze theorem).
Let $a$ be a number and suppose that for all $x\ne a$ in an open interval containing $a$, we have
$$f(x) \le g(x) \le h(x).$$
If
$$\lim_{x\to a} f(x) =  \lim_{x\to a} h(x) = L,$$
then $\lim_{x\to a} g(x)$ exists and equals $L$.

\proof This proof is given in Appendix~F of the textbook, but we include it in the notes as well for
completeness.

Let $\eps > 0$. Since $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$, there is $\delta_1 > 0$ such that every $x$ with
$0<|x-a|<\delta_1$ satisfies
\edef\eqsqueezeone{\the\eqcount}
$$\bigl| f(x) - L\bigr| <\eps.\adveq$$
Likewise, there is $\delta_2>0$ such that every $x$ with
$0<|x-a|<\delta_2$ satisfies
\edef\eqsqueezetwo{\the\eqcount}
$$\bigl| h(x) - L\bigr| <\eps.\adveq$$
Let $\eta>0$ be a number such that for all $x$ with $0<|x-a|<\eta$, we have $f(x)\le g(x)\le h(x)$.
Subtracting $L$ from this inequality gives us
\edef\eqsqueezethree{\the\eqcount}
$$f(x) -L \le g(x) - L \le h(x) - L\adveq$$
for all such $x$. Hence, letting $\delta = \min\{\delta_1,\delta_2,\eta\}$, every $x$ with
$0<|x-a|<\delta$ satisfies all three of~\refeq{\eqsqueezeone},~\refeq{\eqsqueezetwo},
and~\refeq{\eqsqueezethree}. Combining these three inequalities yields
$$-\eps < f(x) -L \le g(x) - L \le h(x) - L<\eps,$$
which implies that
$$\bigl|g(x) - L\bigr| < \eps,$$
as desired.\slug

Near the beginning of the course we encountered the pathological function $\sin(\pi/x)$ and convinced
ourselves that it does not possess a limit as $x\to 0$. However, by combining the squeeze theorem
with Proposition~{\proplimabs}, we can show that a modification of $\sin(\pi/x)$ does in fact approach a
limit.

\proclaim Proposition \advthm. We have
$$\lim_{x\to 0}\, x\sin\Bigl({\pi\over x}\Bigr) = 0.$$

\proof It looks like this follows from the product law, but we cannot actually apply that law because
$\lim_{x\to 0}\sin(\pi/x)$ does not exist.

The thing to note is that the function $\sin$ is bounded from above by the constant function $1$ and
bounded from below by the constant function $-1$. In other words, for all real numbers $t$ we have
$$-1\le \sin(t)\le 1.$$
So for all $x> 0$ we have
$$ -x\le x\sin\Bigl({\pi\over x}\Bigr) \le x $$
and for all $x<0$ we have
$$ x\le x\sin\Bigl({\pi\over x}\Bigr) \le -x.$$
In other words, the inequality
$$ -|x| \le x\sin\Bigl({\pi\over x}\Bigr) \le |x|$$
holds for all $x\ne 0$.
By Proposition~{\proplimabs}, $\lim_{x\to 0} |x| = 0$, so we also have
$\lim_{x\to 0} -|x| = 0$ by the constant multiple law.
Hence by the squeeze theorem (with $f(x) = -|x|$, $g(x) = x\sin(\pi/x)$, and $h(x) = |x|$),
we have
$$\lim_{x\to 0}\, x\sin\Bigl({\pi\over x}\Bigr) = 0.$$
which is what we wanted to show.\slug

We will see more applications of the squeeze theorem in the future, for example, when we work out the derivative
(whatever that is) of the sine function.

\advsect Continuity

\datestamp{18}{ix}
We have spent the entire course so far discussing the limit of functions $f(x)$ at numbers $a$. We now understand
that this is the
value (if any) that the outputs $f(x)$ must approach as $x$ is taken closer and closer to $a$.
This definition of a limit
does not concern itself with $f(a)$ equalling this value that the function approaches,
or in fact equalling any value
at all. But in many cases, $f(a)$ does equal the limit of $f$ at $a$, and this situation turns out to be
pleasant and important enough to warrant significant attention.

We say that a function $f$ is {\it continuous} at a number $a$ if
\medskip
\item{i)} $f(a)$ is defined (i.e., $a$ is in the domain of $f$);
\smallskip
\item{ii)} $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)$ exists; and
\smallskip
\item{iii)} $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = f(a)$.
\medskip
If $f$ is defined near $a$ (on some interval containing $a$ but not necessarily at $a$ itself) and yet fails
to be continuous by violating one or more of these three conditions, then we say $f$ is {\it discontinuous}
at $a$.

Clearly by this definition there are various ways for a function to be discontinuous, and some
of these cases have special names.
\medskip
\item{a)} If $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)$ exists but $f(a)$ is either undefined or does not
equal this limit, we say that $f$ has a {\it removable discontinuity} at $a$, because we could eliminate
this discontinuity by (re)defining the value of $f$ at the single point $a$. For instance,
the function $f(x) = 3x^2 - 2x-1/(x-1)$ we saw before has a removable discontinuity at $x=1$.
\smallskip
\item{b)} If (ii) fails to hold because
$\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = \pm\infty$, then we say $f$ has an {\it infinite discontinuity} at $a$ (regardless
of whether $f(a)$ is defined or not). The function $f(x) = 1/(x-3)^2$ has an infinite discontinuity at
$x=3$.
\smallskip
\item{c)} Lastly, if the one-sided limits of $f$ at $a$ both exist, but (ii) fails because they
do not agree, then we say $f$ has a
{\it jump discontinuity} at $a$---the function ``jumps'' from having some value directly to the left of $a$ to
having some other value directly to the right of $a$. The function
\edef\eqjumpdis{\the\eqcount}
$$f(x) = \cases{0, & if $x\le 0$; \cr 1, & if $x > 0$}\adveq$$
exhibits this behaviour at $x=0$.
\medskip

The appearance of one-sided limits in the definition of a jump discontinuity suggests
that we should have a special name for the general case when
$f(a)$ equals a one-sided limit of $f$ at $a$ but perhaps not the other. We say that a function $f$
is {\it continuous from the right} at a number $a$ if
$$\lim_{x\to a^+} f(x),$$
and {\it continuous from the left} at $a$ if
$$\lim_{x\to a^-} f(x).$$
For example, the
function $f(x)$ appearing in~\refeq{\eqjumpdis} is continuous from the left but not from the right.
Of course, a function is continuous at $a$ if and only if it is continuous from the right and from the left
at $a$, by Theorem~{\thmonesidedsame}.

Since the definition of continuous is given directly in terms of the definition of limit, we can use
the algebraic limit laws in order to prove corresponding algebraic laws for continuity.

\proclaim Theorem \advthm. Suppose that $f$ and $g$ are continuous at $a$. Then
\medskip
\item{i)} $f+g$;
\smallskip
\item{ii)} $f-g$;
\smallskip
\item{iii)} $cf$, where $c$ is any real number; and
\smallskip
\item{iv)} $fg$
\medskip\noindent
are all continuous at $a$. Furthermore, if $g(a)\ne 0$, then
\medskip\noindent
\item{v)} $f/g$
\medskip\noindent
is also continuous at $a$.

\proof We just prove (i), leaving the rest to the reader as they are similarly easy now that we have access
to the algebraic limit laws.

Let $f$ and $g$ be continuous at $a$. This means that
$$\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = f(a)\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad \lim_{x\to a} g(x) = g(a).$$
By the sum law for limits, we have
$$\eqalign{
\lim_{x\to a} (f+g)(x) &= \lim_{x\to a} \bigl(f(x) + g(x)\bigr) \cr
&= \lim_{x\to a} f(x) + \lim_{x\to a} g(x) \cr
&= f(a) + g(a) \cr
&= (f+g)(a),\cr
}$$
which is what we needed to prove.\slug

So if we know certain functions are continuous, then combining them algebraically in the five ways listed
above preserves the property of being continuous. This begs the question of which basic functions
are continuous. We already know about one class of examples, because
the direct substitution property we learned in the previous section directly asserts that any rational function
is continuous at all points in its domain. (Go back and read this theorem and you'll see for yourself!)
In particular, since every polynomial is a rational function and
the domain of a polynomial is the entire real line, polynomials are continuous
everywhere.

The following theorem presents a more exhaustive catalogue of continuous functions.

\proclaim Theorem \advthm. If a function $f$ is a
\medskip
\item{i)} polynomial (e.g., $3x^5 - 4x +1$);
\smallskip
\item{ii)} rational function (e.g., $(4x^2 -3)/(5x-1)$);
\smallskip
\item{iii)} root function (e.g., $\sqrt{x}, \root 3\of x$);
\smallskip
\item{iv)} trigonometric function (e.g., $\sin x$, $\tan x$);
\smallskip
\item{v)} inverse trigonometric function (e.g., $\arcsin x$, $\arccos x$);
\smallskip
\item{vi)} exponential function (e.g., $e^x, 2^x$); or
\smallskip
\item{vii)} logarithmic function (e.g., $\log_{10} x, \ln x$);
\medskip\noindent
then $f$ is continuous at all points in its domain.

\proof We shall skip the proofs of (iv) through (vii) since giving them rigorously would take far too much time,
but as we noted before, (i) and (ii) are simply a restatement of the direct substitution property.
We have also already seen (iii), since limit law (xi) from Theorem~{\thmspeciallimits}
asserts precisely that root functions are continuous.\slug

For any set $S$ of numbers, we say that $f$ is {\it continuous on $S$} if $f$ is continuous at $a$ for all
elements $a$ of $S$. In particular, we say that $f$ is continuous on the interval $(c,d)$ if $f$ is continuous
at $a$ for all $c<a<d$. (Similar definitions can be made for intervals of the form
$(c,d]$, $[c,d)$, and $[c,d]$ by changing $<$ to $\le$ accordingly.)

Knowing which types of functions are continuous now lets us determine many limits without straining our
brain very much at all.

\proclaim Proposition \advthm. We have
$$\lim_{x\to \pi/2} {\sin x - 4\cos x\over 3x} = {2\over 3\pi}.$$

\proof We expand
$$\lim_{x\to \pi/2} {\sin x - 4\cos x\over 3x}
= {\lim_{x\to\pi/2} \sin x - 4\cdot \lim_{x\to \pi/2}\cos x\over \lim_{x\to \pi/2} 3x}$$
by using the algebraic limit laws. In the numerator, we apply the fact that the sine and cosine functions
are continuous,
and in the denominator we use the direct substitution property of the polynomial $3x$. This yields
$$\lim_{x\to \pi/2} {\sin x - 4\cos x\over 3x}
= {\sin(\pi/2) + 4\cos(\pi/2) \over 3\pi/2} = {1+4\cdot 0 \over 3\pi/2} = {2\over 3\pi}.\noskipslug $$

Often we wish to combine functions not just by adding or multiplying them, etc., but instead by {\it composing}
them. For instance, the function $\sin(\pi/x)$ is the composition $f\circ g$ where $f(x) = \sin x$ and
$g(x)=\pi/x$. For this operation we have the following theorem.

\edef\thmcontcomp{\the\thmcount}
\proclaim Theorem \advthm. Suppose that $g(x)$ is continuous at $a$ and that $f(x)$ is continuous at $g(a)$.
Then $f\circ g$ is continuous at $a$; in other words,
$$\lim_{x\to a} (f\circ g)(x) = (f\circ g)(a) = f\bigl( g(a)\bigr).$$

\proof This proof, which proceeds directly via the formal definition of the limit,
is found in Appendix F of the textbook.\slug

This theorem shows that, erratic though its behaviour may be near the vertical line $x=0$, the function
$\sin(\pi/x)$ is continuous at all $a\ne 0$, since it is the composition of the sine function with a
rational function, which is continuous on its domain. We can also use the theorem to compute limits that seem
difficult to compute otherwise.

\proclaim Proposition \advthm. We have
$$\lim_{x\to 1} \arccos\biggl( {1\over \sqrt{x+1}}\biggr) = {\pi\over 4}.$$

\proof
First, we observe that $g(x) = 1/\sqrt{x+1}$ is continuous on its domain.
To see this, note that by Theorem~{\thmcontcomp},
since $\sqrt x$ and $x+1$ are both continuous, their composition
$\sqrt{x+1}$ is, and by the quotient law so is $1/\sqrt{x+1}$ at all points except at $a=-1$.

To apply
Theorem~{\thmcontcomp} again with $f(x)$ and $g(x)$, we must check that $\arccos$ is continuous at
$g(1) = 1/\sqrt{1+1} = 1/\sqrt 2$. Well, $\arccos$ is continuous on its domain, which is the interval
$[-1,1]$, and $1/\sqrt 2$ falls within this interval. So by Theorem~{\thmcontcomp}, we have
$$\lim_{x\to 1} f\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = f\bigl(g(a)\bigr) = \arccos\Bigl({1\over \sqrt 2}\Bigr) = {\pi\over 4},$$
which is what we wanted to show.\slug

\medskip\boldlabel \datestamp{21}{ix} The intermediate value theorem.
The remainder of this section is devoted to
a very important theorem. It expresses the intuitive idea that for a continuous
function to get from one value to another, it must pass through all values in between.

\parenproclaim Theorem {\advthm} (Intermediate value theorem).
Let $f$ be a function that is continuous on some closed interval $[a,b]$ and either
$$f(a) < c < f(b) \qquad\hbox{or}\qquad f(b) < c < f(a),$$
then there is some $x$ in the open interval $(a,b)$ such that $f(x) = c$.

The proof of this theorem is non-trivial and takes several lectures of a second-year math course
to set up properly. In this class we'll simply use it as a black box.

An easy first application is to show that certain polynomials have roots.

\proclaim Proposition \advthm. The polynomial
$$f(x) = 3x^4 - 3x^3 + 4x^2 - 9x + 4$$
has a root.

\proof We wish to show that
$$3x^4 - 3x^3 + 4x^2 - 9x + 4 = 0$$
has a solution. There is a quartic formula (akin to the quadratic formula but for polynomials of degree
$4$), but it is terrifically complicated. One could also try to factorise the polynomial but it is difficult
to figure out where to start.
But we are just tasked with showing that there {\it exists} a root; we are not asked to find out what
the root is exactly. So we can use the intermediate value theorem.

Note that the constant term of the polynomial is $4$, so $f(0) = 4$. This means that at some point, the
graph of the function is above the $x$-axis. Now let's try to find an input that makes $f$ negative. An
easy thing to try is $f(1)$, since we just have to add up all the coefficients:
$$f(1) = 3 - 3 + 4 - 9 + 4 = -1$$
Since $f$ is a polynomial, it is continuous everywhere and in particular is continuous on the interval
$[0,1]$. By the intermediate value theorem with $a=0$ and $b=1$ then, since
$$f(0) = 4 > 0 > -1 = f(1),$$
there must be some $0 < x < 1$ such that $f(x) = 0$. This completes the proof.\slug

Here is a fun ``real-world'' application.

\edef\thmtemp{\the\thmcount}
\parenproclaim Theorem {\advthm} (Temperature theorem).
Show that at any given point in time, there is some location in the world
that has the same temperature as the opposite point on the globe (assume that temperature varies
continuously with position on the globe).

\proof For any point $x$ on the globe, let $f(x)$ be the temperature at that point. Pick any starting point
$p$ and call the opposite point on the globe $-p$. (Strictly speaking, we're now working in $3$-dimensional
space. Think of the centre of the earth as the origin. Then the negative of any point is just its opposite
point on the globe. Don't worry about this point too much. We can still define continuity for functions
on $3$-dimensional space, and the intermediate value theorem still holds on
curves---the equivalent of intervals---in space.)

If $f(p) = f(-p)$, then we're done (the point we sought is just $p$).
If $f(p)\ne f(p')$, then we have to be a bit more clever and invoke the intermediate value theorem.
Let
$$g(x) = f(x) - f(-x)$$
for all points $x$ on the earth. Since $f$ is continuous, by the limit laws $g$ is continuous as well.
For the point $p$ that we chose initially, we have
$$g(p) = f(p) - f(-p) \ne 0.$$
We don't know if $g(p)$ is positive or negative. We just know it's not zero. But now what about $g(-p)$?
It turns out that
$$g(-p) = f(-p) - f\bigl(-(-p)\bigr) = f(-p) - f(p) = -g(p),$$
so whatever sign $g(p)$ had, $g(-p)$ has the opposite sign.

We're almost there. Draw any line on the surface of the earth (this is actually a curve, since the earth
is round)
between $p$ and $-p$. The function $g$ is continuous on this curve, so the intermediate theorem applies.
At one endpoint of the curve, $-p$, the function $g$ has one sign, and at the other endpoint $g$ has
the opposite sign. So by the intermediate value theorem, there must be some point $x$ on this curve
with $g(x) = 0$. But what does this mean for $f$? By the original definition of $g$, we have
$$0 = g(x) = f(x) - f(-x),$$
so $f(x) = f(-x)$, and this point $x$ has the same temperature as its opposite point on the globe.\slug

This theorem applies not just to the temperature, but to any continuous function, and we didn't use the whole
globe, but just a single circle on it. The general version of Theorem~{\thmtemp}
for any function on a sphere in any dimension is called the Borsuk--Ulam theorem; it was first proved in~1933.

\advsect Derivatives

Equipped now with a more robust idea of what it means for a function to approach a value but not necessarily
get there, we revisit the rate-of-change problem introduced in the first lecture, which is
the broad motivating topic for this course as a whole.

Recall that we were considering a function $f$ and wanted to know the rate at which it is changing
near some input $a$ (for now, assume that $f(a)$ is well defined).
We convinced ourselves geometrically that this rate of change could be modelled
as the slope of a line ``tangent'' to the graph of $f(a)$ at the point $\bigl(a,f(a)\bigr)$. We couldn't
rigorously define this line at the time, but it seemed that its slope should be the value of the quotient
$${f(x+h) - f(x)\over h}$$
``when'' $h=0$, even though we knew that $h$ cannot be zero, being in the denominator.

Now that we are comfortable with limits, however, it is abundantly clear to us that we can overcome this
impasse by declaring the slope to be
$$\lim_{h\to 0} {f(a+h) - f(a) \over h},$$
provided this slope is defined. And of course, once we know the slope, we simply remember that this
so-called ``tangent line'' is supposed to touch the function at the point $\bigl(a,f(a)\bigr)$, so we already
know a point on the line. Knowing just one point as well as the slope determines the line completely.
Hence the {\it tangent line} of a function $f$ at the point $P = \bigl(a, f(a)\bigr)$ can be defined to be
the line through $P$ with slope
$$m= \lim_{h\to 0} {f(a+h) - f(a) \over h},$$
so long as this limit exists.

Here's an example demonstrating how to find the equation of a tangent line.

\proclaim Proposition \advthm. For the function
$$f(x) = x^3,$$
the tangent line passing through the point $\bigl( -1, f(-1)\bigr) = (-1, -1)$ is given by the equation
$$y=3x+2,$$
or alternatively
$$3x-y+2 = 0.$$

\proof
To find the slope of the tangent line at $a=-1$, we compute
$$m = \lim_{h\to 0} {f(-1+h) - f(-1)\over h} = \lim_{h\to 0} {(h-1)^3 - (-1)^3\over h}.$$
Expanding $(h-1)^3$ and then simplifying gives us
$$m = \lim_{h\to 0} { h^3 - 3h^2 + 3h -1 - (-1)\over h} = 
\lim_{h\to 0} {h^3 - 3h^2 + 3h \over h},$$
and since
we're only interested in values of $h$ near $0$ but not {\it at} $0$,
we can divide by $h$ on the top and bottom and apply
the direct substitution property to get
$$m = \lim_{h\to 0}(h^2 - 2h + 3) = 0-0+3 = 3.$$
So the slope of the line is $3$ and the equation of the line must be of the form
$$y= 3x+b$$
for some number $b$. We solve for this $b$ by substituting
one known point on the line, namely $(-1,-1)$.
We have
$$-1 = 3(-1) + b,$$
whence $b=2$ and we see that the tangent line can be expressed by the equation
$$3x-y+2 = 0,$$
as claimed.\slug

\medskip\boldlabel \datestamp{23}{ix} Secant lines.
Although one of the primary innovations of differential calculus is the ability to derive tangent lines,
we take a brief detour to talk about secant lines and what they represent.
Suppose $f(x)$ is a function and we graph it on the plane by setting $y=f(x)$. Then $y$ is a quantity that
depends on $x$. If $x$ changes from $x_1$ to $x_2$, then the {\it change in $x$} (also called the
{\it increment} of $x$) is the quantity
$$\Delta x = x_2 - x_1.$$
The change in $y=f(x)$ that corresponds to this change in $x$ is, similarly, denoted
$$\Delta y = f(x_2) - f(x_1).$$
Then the quotient
$${\Delta y\over \Delta x} = {f(x_2) - f(x_1) \over x_2 - x_1}$$
is called the {\it average rate of change of $y$ with respect to $x$} over the interval $[x_1,x_2]$.
Geometrically, this is the slope of the it {\it secant line} that passes through the two points
$\bigl(x_1, f(x_1)\bigr)$ and $\bigl(x_2, f(x_2)\bigr)$.
We already saw above that the slope of the tangent line is the limit of the slopes of secant lines as
the distance between the two points goes to $0$. The majority of this course will be about the manifold
applications of this definition.

But for some problems, the answer lies directly in the simpler calculation associated with the secant line.
Take this one for instance. The fuel usage of a car is often measured in litres per hundred kilometres
(so a smaller number means better fuel economy).
Suppose that between kilometre $20$ and kilometre $320$ on Highway~40, your car goes from a full tank
($40$ litres) down to $19$ litres of gasoline in that time.
If we wish to know the average
rate of change of $g$ with respect to $d$, then we divide
$${\Delta g\over \Delta d} = {40-19\over 320-20} = {21\ \hbox{litres}\over 300\ \hbox{kilometres}}
= {7\ \hbox{litres}\over 100\ \hbox{kilometres}}$$
to conclude that the average rate of fuel consumption is $7\;\hbox{L/100km}$.

If we had a fuller description or a graph of the function of fuel usage with respect to time, then we'd
be able to take secant lines cutting the graph at closer and closer points. In the limit, we'd obtain
a tangent line, whose slope indicates the fuel usage at a specific given point, but this idea of
{\it instantaneous} rate of change of fuel with respect to distance has very little real-world meaning, so we
omit it.

(Well, that's not true. The instananeous rate of change of
fuel usage with respect to distance does have some real-world meaning, although the interpretation
is perhaps more amusing than actually useful.
A litre equals one thousandth of a cubic metre, and a kilometre is of course a thousand metres. So we
see that the unit $\hbox{L/100}\,\hbox{km}$ equals $10^{-8}\,\hbox{m}^2$ or $0.01\,\hbox{mm}^2$---it measures
area! This might be
baffling the first time you see it,
but the best way to interpret this, borrowing numbers from the previous
example, is to imagine all $21$ litres of fuel (which is three-dimensional)
stretched out over the $300\,\hbox{km}$ stretch of highway as a very long skinny rectangular prism. The
average fuel usage, then, is the average cross-sectional area of this rectangular prism---namely $0.007$
square millimetres in the example.  If we now
imagine the amount of fuel used varying smoothly as the driver manages the throttle, then the thickness
of this imaginary long rectangular prism of liquid varies along the drive as well, and
the instantaneous rate of change of fuel with respect to distance at any point is exactly the cross-sectional
area of the rectangular prism at that point.)

\medskip\boldlabel Instantaneous velocity.
A far more useful application of the concept of instantaneous rate of change is when calculating
distance travelled over time. This is the notion of {\it speed} or {\it velocity}, which we are well
acquainted with from a young age.

Let $s(t)$ be the position of some object at time $t$.
We deduced earlier that, whatever the instantaneous velocity is at some time $a$,
it can be approximated by taking averages
$${s(a+h) - s(a) \over h},$$
over smaller and smaller time intervals $h$. Now that we know what a limit is, we can simply {\it define}
the {\it instantaneous velocity} at some time $t=a$ of an object with position function $s(t)$ to be
\edef\eqvdef{\the\eqcount}
$$v(a) = \lim_{h\to 0} {s(a+h) - s(a) \over h}.\adveq$$

This is best illustrated by an example.
Ha Ling Peak in the Rocky Mountains is 2407 metres above sea level, and the town
of Canmore below it is at roughly 1400 metres above sea level. (Let's say it's an even 1400 metres just
for the sake of the example.)
At the top of the peak there is a sign
telling you not to throw rocks off the cliff as they could hit someone down below and cause grave injury.
Suppose you could throw a rock off Ha Ling Peak
with enough horizontal velocity to make it to town. Let's find out what its
vertical velocity would be once it reached the ground, ignoring factors such as air drag, etc.

The elevation of the rock after $t$ seconds of free fall is modelled by the function
$$s(t) = 2407 - 4.9 t^2,$$
assuming its initial vertical velocity is zero, and it starts falling from 2407 metres above sea level.
First, we find out how long it takes for the rock to reach the ground in town, which is at 1400 metres.
We need to solve the equation
$$1400 = 2407 - 4.9 t^2;$$
this rearranges to
$$t = \sqrt{{1400\over 4.9}} \approx 14.3$$
seconds.

So we want to calculate $v(14.3)$, where $v(a)$ is the instantaneous velocity at time $t=a$. First we need
to derive a formula for $v$. Plugging our formula for $s$ into the definition~\refeq{\eqvdef} of $v$,
we solve the resulting limit to obtain
$$\eqalign{
v(a) &= \lim_{h\to 0} {2407 - 4.9(a+h)^2 -(2407 - 4.9a^2)\over h} \cr
&= \lim_{h\to 0} {-4.9a^2 - 9.8 ah - h^2 + 4.9a^2\over h} \cr
&= \lim_{h\to 0} {-9.8 ah - h^2 \over h} \cr
&= \lim_{h\to 0} (-9.8 a - h) \cr
&= -9.8a. \cr
}$$
The negative sign here simply expresses that the rock will be moving downwards (losing elevation). From here
we can simply plug in $a=14.3$ to conclude that the vertical velocity of the rock right before it hits the ground
is
$$v(14.3) = -9.8(14.3) = -140.5$$
metres per second. (Of course in real life this could not happen because the terminal velocity of the rock
is likely much lower than this, and you would have to throw the rock at well over 100 metres per second to
even reach the town in the first place. Also because you would never do this for fear of landing in Alberta
jail.)

\medskip\boldlabel The derivative.
In the situation above, the equation of the tangent line was not totally
necessary to solve the corresponding motivating problem. We only needed to know the slope of this line.
Hence for a given function $f(x)$ we have a special name for the slope
\edef\eqderivativedef{\the\eqcount}
$$\lim_{h\to 0} {f(a+h) - f(a)\over h}\adveq$$
of the tangent line at the input $a$;
it is called the {\it derivative} of the function $f$ at $a$, and denoted $f'(a)$. Sometimes it's useful
to write $x=a+h$, so that
$$f'(a) = \lim_{x\to a} {f(x) - f(a)\over x-a}.$$
This is a completely equivalent
alternative definition of the derivative which we'll use from time to time, but more often
than not, the definition~\refeq{\eqderivativedef} leads to simpler calculations.

Here's an example.

\proclaim Proposition \advthm. Let
$$f(x) = x^2 - x + 1.$$
We have $f'(3) = 5$, and more generally for every number $a$ we have
$$f'(a) = 2a-1.$$

\proof If we do the derivation for general $a$, we can then just plug in $a=3$ to get the answer of
$2(3) - 1 = 5$. But let's do the case $a=3$ first just to get a sense of the calculations, then tackle
the general case after.

We have
$$\eqalign{
f'(3) &= \lim_{h\to 0} {f(3+h) - f(3)\over h} \cr
&= \lim_{h\to 0} {(3+h)^2 - (3+h) + 1 - (3^2 - 3 + 1)\over h} \cr
&= \lim_{h\to 0} {3^2 + 6h + h^2 - 3-h + 1 - 3^2 + 3 - 1)\over h} \cr
&= \lim_{h\to 0} {5h + h^2 \over h} \cr
&= \lim_{h\to 0} (5+h) \cr
&= 5+0 \cr
&= 5,\cr
}$$
and for the general case at $a$, we simply replace $3$ with $a$ everywhere to obtain
$$\eqalign{
f'(a) &= \lim_{h\to 0} {f(a+h) - f(a)\over h} \cr
&= \lim_{h\to 0} {(a+h)^2 - (a+h) + 1 - (a^2 - a + 1)\over h} \cr
&= \lim_{h\to 0} {a^2 + 2ah + h^2 - a - h + 1 - a^2 + a - 1)\over h} \cr
&= \lim_{h\to 0} {2ah - h + h^2\over h} \cr
&= \lim_{h\to 0} (2a - 1 + h) \cr
&= 2a-1 \cr
}$$
instead.\slug

\medskip\boldlabel The derivative as a function.
The second calculation in the previous proposition completely characterised the values of $f'(a)$ for different
values of $a$. This, along with the notation $f'$ we employed, suggests that $f'$ is itself a function---plug
in different inputs $a$ and you get different outputs. We like to indicate inputs of functions by $x$,
so substituting $x$ for $a$ in the definition~\refeq{\eqderivativedef} of the derivative, we obtain
\edef\eqfprime{\the\thmcount}
$$f'(x) = \lim_{h\to 0} {f(x+h) - f(x) \over h},\adveq$$
wherever this limit exists.
Hence taking the derivative or {\it differentiating} the function $f$ resulted in another function, $f'$.

\bye

Let us calculate the derivatives of several simple functions, to get a sense of this idea.

\proclaim Proposition \advthm.
The derivative of
$$f(x) = 2x^3 - x$$
is
$$f'(x) = 6x^2 - 1.$$

\proof We simply compute
$$\eqalign{
f'(x) &= \lim_{h\to 0} {f(x+h) - f(x)\over h} \cr
&= \lim_{h\to 0} {2(x+h)^3 - (x+h) - (2x^3 -x)\over h} \cr
&= \lim_{h\to 0} {2(x^3 + 3x^2h + 3xh^2 + h^3) - x -h - 2x^3 + x\over h} \cr
&= \lim_{h\to 0} {2x^3 + 6x^2h + 6xh^2 + 2h^3 -h - 2x^3 \over h} \cr
&= \lim_{h\to 0} {6x^2h + 6xh^2 + 2h^3 -h \over h} \cr
&= \lim_{h\to 0} (6x^2 + 6xh + 2h^2 -1) \cr
&= 6x^2 - 1.\noskipslug \cr
}$$

Recall that the derivative $f'(x)$ is only defined to exist at points $x$ for which the limit~\refeq{\eqfprime}
exists. This sometimes means that the domain of a derivative is smaller than the domain of the original
function.

\edef\propsqrtderiv{\the\thmcount}
\proclaim Proposition \advthm. The function
$$f(x) = \sqrt x$$
is defined on $[0,\infty)$, but its derivative, which is given by the formula
$$f'(x) = {1\over 2\sqrt x},$$
is only defined on $(0,\infty)$.

\proof It is clear that $\sqrt x$ is well defined for all $x\ge 0$. We now calculate
$$\eqalign{
f'(x) &= \lim_{h\to 0} {\sqrt{x+h} - \sqrt x \over h} \cr
&=\lim_{h\to 0} {\sqrt{x+h} - \sqrt x \over h}\cdot {\sqrt{x+h} + \sqrt x\over \sqrt{x+h} + \sqrt x} \cr
&=\lim_{h\to 0} {x+h-x \over h\bigl(\sqrt{x+h} + \sqrt x\bigr)} \cr
&=\lim_{h\to 0} {1 \over \sqrt{x+h} + \sqrt x} \cr
&={1 \over 2\sqrt x}, \cr
}$$
as claimed. But we see that this is undefined for $x=0$ since we cannot have $0$ in the denominator, so we
conclude that $0$ is not in the domain of $f'$, which proves the final assertion in the proposition.\slug

\medskip\boldlabel Notes on notation. Suppose we use the notation $y=f(x)$ to show that the variable $y$ depends on
the variable $x$. Then some common alternative ways to denote the derivative $f'(x)$ are
$$ f'(x) = {dy\over dx} = {d\over dx} f(x) = y' = {df\over dx} = Df(x) = D_x f(x) = \dot y,$$
sorted from left to right in rough descending order of how often you'll see these notations used in these
notes.

The reason that there are so many different ways of writing down the derivative symbolically is largely
historical; the theory of differential calculus was built up slowly over centuries,
and the notations mathematicians came up with along the way were well suited for different contexts and
purposes.
Differential calculus was first invented in the late 1600s by Isaac Newton and Gottfried
Wilhelm Leibniz independently (Leibniz's account was published first, but Newton's private notes predated
those of Leibniz by several years). Leibniz used the notation $dy/dx$, while Newton favoured the ``flyspeck''
notation $\dot y$. We will not use the latter notation very much but it appears often in physics literature.
The {\it prime notation} $f'(x)$ is probably the most popular notation in modern times.
It was first used by Leonhard Euler but popularised by
Joseph-Louis Lagrange, and first appeared in print in 1749.

Returning to the Leibniz notation $dy/dx$, we stress that this should not be regarded as a ratio; it is simply
another way of writing $f'(x)$. It is reminiscent of the expression $\Delta y/\Delta x$ for the rate of change,
which is apt because
$${dy\over dx} = \lim_{\Delta x\to 0} {\Delta y\over \Delta x}.$$
The symbol $d/dx$ is called the {\it differentiation operator}, because you can apply it to a function $f$
to obtain its derivative $(df/dx)$. (Recall that the operation of taking a derivative is called differentiation.)
The term {\it operator} refers to a special type of function that takes as input a function and outputs another
function, which is what $d/dx$ does, in an abstract sense.
When we evaluate the function $f'$ at some number $a$, it is easy to write $f'(a)$ for this value. It is
a little more cumbersome to write this number if we're using Leibniz notation; we write instead
$${dy\over dx}\bigg|_{x=a}\qquad\hbox{or}\qquad {dy\over dx} \biggr]_{x=a}.$$

\medskip\boldlabel Differentiability. A function $f$ is said to be {\it differentiable at some number} $a$
if $f'(a)$ (which is defined in terms of a limit) exists. It is said to be {\it differentiable} on a set
$S$ if $f$ is differentiable at every number in $S$. (In practice, the set $S$ is most often taken to be an
open interval $(a,b)$, an interval of the form $(a,\infty)$ or $(-\infty,b)$, or else the whole real line.
If it's differentiably on the whole real line, we might say $f$ is {\it differentiable everywhere}.)

We already saw in Proposition~{\propsqrtderiv}
that the function $\sqrt x$, though it is defined on $[0,\infty)$, is only differentiable on $(0,\infty)$.
We now proceed to consider a couple more examples.

\proclaim Proposition \advthm. The function
$$f(x) = |x|$$
is not differentiable at $x=0$. However, it is differentiable at every other real number.

\proof We wish to calculate
$$f'(0) = \lim_{h\to 0} {|0+h| - |0| \over h} = \lim_{h\to 0} {|h|\over h}.$$
But we have encountered this limit on the right-hand side before! It was the subject of
Proposition~{\proplimdne}, and our very first example of a limit that does not exist.
Thus $f'(0)$ does not exist.

To prove the second assertion, assume that $x\ne 0$ and show that $f'(x)$ does exist.

This brings up an important point. The function $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous at $0$, but not differentiable
there. Therefore continuity at a point does not guarantee differentiability at that point. However,
the opposite implication does hold.

\proclaim Theorem \advthm. Suppose $f$ is a function and $a$ is a number such that $f(a)$ is defined.
If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then it is continuous at $a$.

\proof Assume that $f'(a)$ exists; that is, the limit
$$\lim_{x\to a} {f(x) - f(a)\over x-a}$$
equals some well defined number.
Then we have
$$\eqalign{
\lim_{x\to a} f(x) &= \lim_{x\to a} f(x) - f(a) + f(a) \cr
&= \lim_{x\to a} \biggl({f(x) - f(a)\over x-a}\cdot (x-a)\biggr) + f(a) \cr
&= \lim_{x\to a} {f(x) - f(a)\over x-a}\cdot \lim_{x\to a} (x-a) + f(a) \cr
&= f'(a) \cdot 0 + f(a) \cr
&= f(a), \cr
}$$
which is exactly the criterion for $f$ to be continuous at $a$.\slug
