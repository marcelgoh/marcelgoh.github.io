31 August
Today we spent the first half of the class going over the syllabus and fretting about various administrative things that don't matter. For the second half of the class, we pondered the idea of the "rate of change" of a function "at" a given point. We saw how taking successive approximations can get us closer and closer to this "instantaneous rate of change."

2 September
We started today's class by thinking about what we would like our definition of a function being close to something as its input approaches something to have. Then we stated this definition: that of a limit. It was a long definition that is hard to digest, so we dwelt on the definition for quite some time. Then we proved that the limit of a certain rational function equals 4 when its input is close to 1, directly from the definition (without using any fancy laws or whatnot).

4 September
We continued thinking about the concept of limit this class. We stated formally what it means for a function not to equal a given number, then interpreted the very definition of limit as the result of a game between ourselves and an all-powerful Adversary. We also defined what it means for the limit to not exist. Our running example throughout this entire class was the function |x|/x, and we defined the concept of one-sided limits. Lastly, we learned that if the one-sided limits exist but do not agree at a given point, then the limit does not exist there.

9 September
Today we started by establishing the fact that if the left- and right-hand limits of a function both exist and are equal to one another, then the bona fide limit of that function equals that limit too. This is a direct consequence of the definitions so we did not dwell on it too long. The bulk of this class was spent defining vertical asymptotes and what it means for a function to go to infinity or minus infinity.

11 September
We discussed limits at infinity, including infinite limits at infinity. We considered a couple of examples, then moved on to a new section on algebraic limit laws.

14 September
We began today with a proof sketch of the sum law for limits, then saw various examples of the limit laws in action. The examples we discussed involved the use of the direct substitution rule, sometimes by invoking a theorem that says that if two functions f and g agree in some neighbourhood of a (except possibly at a itself), then their limits at a must be equal. We saw an example of using one-sided limits to our advantage before noting a rule for limits at infinity that we'll use next class.

16 September
We practised evaluating limits at infinity by trying our hand at a few examples, then introduced the squeeze theorem and gave an application. This was the last lecture devoted solely to limits.

18 September
Today's topic was continuity. We defined what it means for a function to be continuous, and then listed some limit laws and catalogued basic examples of continuous functions. We saw that knowing which functions are continuous can help us compute some limits quickly. We convinced ourselves that the composition of two continuous functions is continuous, and used this theorem to compute an example.

21 September
We spent the first half of the class discussing the intermediate value theorem. We used it to prove that there are always two opposite points in the world with the same temperature. Then we moved on to defining the tangent line. We computed the tangent line for a simple cubic function.

23 September
We burned some gas in our car and then threw a rock off a mountain. After that we defined the derivative and calculated the derivative of an example function.

25 September
First we proved that sinx / x goes to 1 as x goes to 0, a fact that strictly speaking does not belong to Chapter 2 but which you'll need for your WebWork problems. Then we continued discussing the concept of the derivative of a function itself being a function. We defined what it means for a function to be differentiable at a number, saw some examples, and proved that differentiability implies continuity. Lastly we introduced the definition of a higher derivative and squared away some notation. With this lecture done we have officially covered everything in Chapter 2. Tonight there is a full moon.

28 September
Today we started building our collection of differentiation rules. We stated the sum rule, difference rule, and constant-multiple rule, then proved the power rule for derivatives. We worked through an example, then finished off the class by stating and proving the product rule.

30 September
We proved the reciprocal rule and used it to prove a general quotient rule for functions. Then we tried to find the derivative of exponential functions and gave a (non-numerical) definition of the constant e.

2 October
First we practised using the product and quotient rules in an example that also involved the exponential function. Then we set our sights on finding the derivatives of the six trigonometric functions. The sine and cosine functions we differentiated directly from the limit definition of a derivative. The remaining four we dealt with using the reciprocal and quotient rules. Lastly we considered (very) high derivatives of the sine and cosine functions.

7 October
Today's class was all about the chain rule. We reasoned about why a product of derivatives must show up, then stated the formal theorem. We used it to derive the cosine double angle formula from the sine one, then gave an alternative "proof" of the Pythagorean theorem for sine and cosine. (We discussed why this is actually an example of circular reasoning and thus not an actual proof.) Then we computed the derivatives of general exponential functions, and reminded ourselves what definitions like "inverse" and "one-to-one" mean, which will be very relevant for next class (Friday after the break), when we compute derivatives of inverse functions.

16 October
This was our first class back from a long restorative break. We pictorially derived a formula for the derivative of the inverse of a function. Then we used this to calculate the derivative of the natural logarithm, nth roots, and inverse trigonometric functions.

19 October
Today we discussed the history of the Mercator projection, its relation to the secant function, and how the associated open problem motivated the developments of calculus in the middle of the seventeenth century. Then we found some tangent lines to the unit circle with some prescribed slope, first without implicit differentiation, and lastly with implicit differentiation. We only just had time to mention what implicit differentiation is, but we'll cover it in much more detail next class.

21 October
We spent the first portion of class analysing this elliptic curve. Then we discussed the wondrous application of elliptic curve theory to cryptography. We finished off the class by rederiving some old formulas implicitly, including (at last!) a general version of the power rule that works for all real powers.

23 October
We started by seeing how taking the logarithm of both sides of an equation before implicitly differentiating can make our life easier sometimes. Then we noticed that when we implicitly differentiate things, we often end up with equations involving a variable y and its derivatives. These are called differential equations, and we found the general solution to a very simple one during this lecture. We then started trying to tackle a harder one involving the second derivative, but only got partway through before class ended and we had to part ways.

26 October
We finished our proof that any function which is the negative of its second derivative must be some linear combination of the sine and cosine functions. We used this to derive a very famous identity of Euler concerning the square root i of –1. Then we asked ourselves which functions are equal to their second derivatives. This led us to examine the shape of a wire strung between two poles. By analysing the physics involved, we arrived at a formula for y in terms of x that describes this shape exactly. This led to the definitions of the hyperbolic cosine and hyperbolic sine, which we didn't have much time to play around with. We'll learn more about these hyperbolic functions next class.

28 October
Today we saw that the derivative of hyperbolic sine is hyperbolic cosine and vice versa, which means that both of these functions are equal to their second derivative. Then we asserted (but did not prove) that any function equal to its second derivative must be a linear combination of hyperbolic sine and cosine. We embarked on a linear-algebraic digression about how the set of all functions satisfying a differential equation often forms a vector space. The dimension is equal to the highest derivative in the differential equation. Then we introduced the other four hyperbolic functions, proved some identities regarding them, and found their derivatives. We finished off by defining the inverse hyperbolic functions and showing that you can (with a bit of work) derive their formulas explicitly and (with less work) figure out their derivatives.

30 October
We had a fairly chill lecture today. We observed that if two or more variables are related by some equation, then differentiating both sides of the equation with respect to time gives an equation that relates all the rates of change of these variables. We applied this principle to a problem involving traffic radar and a problem involving a conical water tower dispensing water. Then we analysed this scene from a 1979 James Bond movie.
